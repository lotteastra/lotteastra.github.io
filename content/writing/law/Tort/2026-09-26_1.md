1. The tort of negligence (of the sort which occurred in Hoyle v Hampshire CC [2022] EWHC 934 (QB) (covered in the relevant lecture schema)) is not explicitly referred to in the FAA. So, why is it covered, as a tort that can give rise to FAA damages?

Section 1(1) of the Fatal Accidents Act 1976 provides that 

'any wrongful act, neglect or default which is such as would (if death had not ensued) have entitled the person injured to maintain an action and recover damages in respect thereof, the person who would have been liable if death ahd not ensued shall be liable to an action for damages nonwithsdatning the death of the person injured.'

Therefore the requirements are that there is a 'wrongful act, neglect or default,' which is the case in Hoyle, equally the claim is made by dependents and in section 1(2) the provision is for dependants which is defined in the next section. Therefore the act provides a means for families in a certain situation to bring an action under a general list without needing to do so under a specific tort written out in the act. 

2. Under whatsub-section are funeral expenses of the deceased recoverable under an FAA action? How should that sub-section be written?

Section 3(5) provides that 
"(5)If the dependants have incurred funeral expenses in respect of the deceased, damages may be awarded in respect of those expenses." 

It is tied specifically to the expenses by the dependents rather than the expenses of the funeral more generally, and it gives a great deal of discretion to the court. I think that it could be rewritten to make it so it is the general expenses of the funeral may be considered with damages being provided to cover some amount of the cost, but the discretion should exist so as not to over compensate an overly lavish funeral. 

3. Could a nurse who cared for an old lady for six years prior to her death, and who lived with the old lady as a trusted nurse/companion, recover any award under the FAA? Why, or why not?

Section 1(3)(a-g) provides who may recover under the FAA 1976 as a dependant with Section 1A providing who can claim for bereavement. Under Section 1(3)(b)(i-iii) the nurse could claim to be a dependant if they were living as the husband or wife of the deceased. While they might not have been married the degree of care could potentially be reaching the threashold of living 'as the husband or wife of the deceased,' though the fact that she was a nurse and doing this for financial conisderation rather than the natural love and affection between a spouse could make that an unworkable line of argument. Ultimately it is unlikely that they could claim through Section 1(3) as a dependant on account that they lack a familial connection. The same argument could be applied to Section 1A but it is also unlikely to suceed as that subsection does call for 'wife or husband,' specifically.

2. This case was brought by the administrator of the deceased’s estate. That administrator included an FAA claim on Ms Kotke’s behalf. Why is that allowed?

Because only the executor can bring a claim but they bring claims on behalf of all those who can claim to be dependants under Section 2(1) FAA 1976. 

3. What was the significance of Ms Kotke and the deceased sharing shopping expenses?

The case pointed out a number of prior authorities which talked about how the requirement for a household was not sharing a physical house but about the 'centre of gravity,' with the couple, and therefore the shared expenses constituted evidence that there was a shared household which was relevant for the statute. 

4. Ultimately, why did Ms Kotke fail in her FAA claim?

Because the shift in the 'centre of gravity,' did not occur until within the 2 year period when she became pregnant. Therefore the appeal was dismissed.

5. Do you think that the outcome was fair? Why, or why not? 

I think the outcome did follow the law but it demonstrated a lack of courage on behalf of the judge. In particular a failure of the common law to meet where society was heading and interpreting an old statute in light of changing societal mores. The 1976 Act was designed iterating on prior acts, tailored to the kinds of relationships people had in the past, but as time has passed more and more couples are choosing to cohabit without marriage. The section requiring 2 years may no doubt have been revolutionary at the time, and was asking the question of if the relationship was genuine. However, I think in the modern day as relationship mores have changed, with about a quarter of those in relationships choosing to cohabit without marriage the common law should be ready to meet those changes. It should not be forgotten that the original purpose of the FAA and its proceeding acts was to address a policy failure with widows facing destitution as a result of their husbands dying. As time has passed however the nature of marriage has shifted. The requirement should be proof of a genuine and enduring relationship at the time of the accident, which gave rise to dependency. I think as women are more in the workplace it should not be presumed that all people in relationships would be dependents, and the economic rationale should be shifted and developed. Though a change of this nature would require statutory change.
