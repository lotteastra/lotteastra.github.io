Causation = 51% probability then passes the balance of probabilities. 

Bull v. Devon -> Split campus hospital case. If the breach caused by the hospital. In twins depending on the situation one foetus might be weaker inside the womb, and that might cause brain damage as a result in an extreme case. The judge considered that the delay caused it with 100% certainty in first instance. CA held that 51% chance. Geting over the balance of probabilities is crucial.

Hobson v. East Berkshire -> No fractional damages in the UK. Schoolboy who fell out of a tree in his schoolyard. Was not properly examined at the hospital. The hospital showed a necrotic joint on second presentation after 5 days. Two possible reasons, the impact ruptured them and that would be innocent. Argued that the delay was the reason. Two reasons the innocent reason and the breach, the judge held that it was a 25% chance that it was caused by breach and therefore 0% damage.

McGee -> John Mcgee worked for the National Coal board, he made bricks, that oven was hot and sweaty and dusty. In the course of the day the dust would adhere to his skin. Regulatory requirement that there should be showers after work. That innocent brick dust accumulated, but when it was still stuck to him on the 3 mile travel from work to home. He suffered industrial dermititis. DoC was conceded and breach. Question of if the guilty dust or the innocent dust. Had the showers been provided then would the harm not been suffered? Evidence couldn't be established. HoL Came up with a 5 section way to explain this.

Fairchild -> Working in construction and exposed to asbestos by employer number 1, but he had been exposed to asbestos in every single subsequent employer, he was suffering from melosthememoma, no way of telling when the dust that triggered it was deposited. So D tried to argue that it was not their dust, but could rely on material risk.

Barker v. Corus -> Employers and exposed to asbestos dust multiple times, but also exposed during self employment. Doesn't deprive use of that McGee causal link.

1. There was only one agent that the claimant could have been exposed to

2. D failed to take precautions against that element

3. This breach proceeded C suffering that precise harm

4. Cannot prove at the moment that C suffered harm directly, but they can establish that their risk was materially increased

5. Where there are multiple agents they are to be judged separately.

Supersaturated oxygen case -> Suffered from so many problems from being a premature baby. Any of those problems could have caused blindness. These were different agents. A failure to take preventative measures against one out of five possible causes is no evidence of that specific cause.

Edwards v. Two Sisters -> Principle has only been used in exposure to asbestos, but it may be used in other cases and may be used well beyond.

Barker v. Corus -> Conundrum where prior employers might have gone bust. What is the solvent defendant liable for in damage. 

Several liability -> Several defenders and each one is liable for 100% of damages

Joint liability -> Where D can seek a contribution from the other defendants and responsibility can be jointly allocated in proportion in relation to wrongdoing

You allege both at the same time. Typical basis of liability

Proportionate liability -> Where D is liable for their proportion of damages

Very important for liability. C may be only be able to go after one, Under McGee who pays what. 

Barker v. Corus -> Split decision but majority found that the liability is porportionate. Majority states that establishing causation exceptionally but C suffers a little bit so a quid pro quo. Lord Rodger said it should be retained. Unions were irritated. Compensation act of 2006. They lobbied and S.3 added and reversed Barker, where the responsible employee shall be jointly and severablely liable. S.3 is headed melisthemioma damages and it is only reversed for that one element. So Barker v. Corus is generally the law of the land.

Costello -> Worked for a company for many years as a secretary at Ellsemere port, and people in the environment were more exposed, and McGee applies even where the exposure was innocent, environmental contamination. Very powerful and rescued a lot of employees.

Chester v. Afshar -> Failure to warn problem. Operate in the counterfactual, would the damage happened anyway. Patient would say if warned they would not have gone through with it, you would have to be a saint to answer honestly. Causation assessed by an objective subjective test. Listen to the patient, and assess if it is objectively honest. C said that she would pause and consider it. HoL had to consider it. Judges were split. Most senior judges dissented she said she would have needed it anyway so causation would not have been established, and a pause would not have changed things as it is an inherent risk. Majority held it anyway. The right of the patient and rewards the honest patient. Fair to uphold because it was the precise injury that should have been warned against. Reaffirming the right of the patient to decide.

Novus Actus Itravenous

Where there is an act in between. Do these intervening acts sever the chain.

Core v. IBC -> So unlikely to be almost unforseeable would sever the chain.

Knightley v. Johns -> Inevitable accidents, one way tunnel in Birmingham, D1 overturned his car, police called and they were told to close the tunnel immediately but the police officer didn't close it he is D2 so he gets one of his PCs to go down the tunnel wrong way on a motorbike, and hits D3. D3 fell out because inevitable accident. Did the failure to shut the tunnel prevent liability from D1 to C, and that was novus actus because so unlikely a police officer would close the tunnel. 

Not so common that the claimant would fall out for NAI nowadays in cases like that. Now.

Vicars Golding -> Concerned about fire extinguishers in the medieval church, that are dry powder, 3 teenage boys sprayed things in the church, and sued the fire extinguisher for using a type that was so hazardous for the church. Argued NAI and held that it was NAI. So unlikely as to be almost unforseeable. 

Can C sever their chain?

Mckew v. Holland -> Work accident hip and leg affected. He was looking for a flat, he went down the external staircase with his 2 year old toddler, fell on his weak leg, and was the employer liable for the extra damage, held that NAI severed liability for that part of it. Claimant may sever. 

Webon v. Barclays Bank -> Woman with polio and left with a limp. In a lot of pain and worked at Barclay's bank, fell on a protruding stone, hyper-extended leg, and tried to manage it, so she sought a surgeon. Said that nothing could be done conservatively, and amputated it, but found that there was better options surgically. Question, was the recommendation NAI, it was held not to be, because medical negligence is not so unlikely as to be unforseeable. Very difficult for medical negligence to sever the chain. Allocated responsibility.
