Vadia and her husband, Wayne, conceived a child, Zara, who was born with severe disabilities. It is not clear whether, or to what extent, these disabilities were caused by a severe knock on her stomach which Vadia received when playing netball whilst five months pregnant, or whether the disabilities were caused by a morning sickness medication prescribed for Vadia by Dr Yest. That medication also caused a painful rash on Vadia’s torso and elevated blood pressure for Vadia which themselves required urgent medical treatment.

Meanwhile, Wayne required a dental operation to correct a misaligned jaw. He sought advice from Dr Abe, and agreed to undergo a realignment. He was not warned of the risk of suffering a permanent numbness of part of his face (which was assessed at 1%). Wayne underwent the operation, and was left with permanent damage to his saliva glands (which was a minute risk of 0.005%, which the parties agree did not have to be disclosed to Wayne). Wayne admits that, in his job as an actor, the appearance of his misaligned jaw caused him some difficulties in obtaining lead parts, and that he had long wished to have it corrected. 

On the way home from a theatre performance once night, Wayne suffered the misfortune of being attacked whilst trying to shield his female colleague and himself from a violently-disturbed man who they encountered outside the theatre. That man, Gregory, was a patient of Dr Inglis (a psychiatrist employed by the Health NHS Trust), and was being treated for a serious psychotic condition. After extensive in-patient treatment, Gregory had been permitted to live in a ‘community half-way house’, on the undertaking that he would report to the psychiatric out-patients unit of the Health NHS Trust every month, and would undergo regular blood-testing to verify that he was taking his prescribed medication. 

However, Gregory had not attended that out-patients unit for two months at the time of the attack on Wayne, and his absence had not been followed up by Dr Inglis. The theatre outside where the attack happened was only three doors away from the half-way house where Gregory resided.

Advise the parties as to whether any of them is owed a duty of care

ZARA v. DR. YEST -> Liability can exist under the Congenital Disabilities Act 1976. A doctor does owe a duty of care, Robinson recognized category. S.1(5) CDA.1976 states that doctors cannot be liabile for omissions, however we have an affirmative action. Equally the child was born alive which therefore means that the conditions for the CDA.1976 are established. Drs owe 4 duties as part of their recongized category, duty to: Diagnose, treat, refer, warn. These four duties encompass the recognized doctor patient category. This is captured by treat. 

Checklist for CDA liability 

1. C is born: Zara was born alive S.1(1) S.4(2) 'a baby is part of its mother; there is up to that point, a single legal person,' regarding pre birth, YAH v. Medway NHS Foundation Trust. 'The baby if born alive, has its own set of rights dervied from [the act],' Mulheron R, quoting Zeromska Smith v. United Lincolnshire Hospitals NHS Trust.

2. With a disability: S.4(1) defined as being born with 'any deformity, disease or abnormality,' qualified that it cannot be for things that C believes are objectionable A (A minor) v. A Health and Social Services Trust 'in a modern civilized society the colour of their skin = no more than the colour of their eyes or their hair, or their intelligence or their height - cannot and should not count as connoting some damage.' In this case it is described as a 'severe disability and therefore captured.

3. As a result of an occurance: Questionable based on the fact we do not know what was the main cause for the disability unless the drug can be found to have been 'an occurance,' which causes the injury then it will not be able to find liability. 

4. Unnecessary to show that there was injury to the mother, merely that there was a duty owed by D to C at the time of the occurance. Given that Robinson recognized category doctor patient, and there are four recognized duties by a doctor to a patient (Diagnose, treat, refer, warn. These four duties encompass the recognized doctor patient category.) This would be captured by 'treat.' Possibly also warn.

Duty was owed most likey but there is a question on if that duty was actually breached.

VADIA v. DR. YEST -> Potential negligence in failure to warn or treat. There was a duty owed Robinson recognized category, but contingent on warnings, were there any, and if those warnings were sufficient, and if the treatment was negligent or not. 

(Vadia also having a claim against the drug manufacturer question?)

ZARA v. VADIA -> Precluded by statute Congenital Disabilities Act 1976 S.1(1) "(other than the child’s own mother)" Exception for road traffic accidents under S.2, but this is not a road traffic accident where the mother is driving. 1978 pearson report questioned why the mother should be immune, and there is scope to argue, but given the clear words of the statute it is very very unlikely that a duty of care could be owed from Vadia to Zara. 

WAYNE v. DR. ABE -> Captured by one of the 4 duties from doctor to patient in particular duty to warn. Doctors have a duty to warn about objectively significant, and subjectively significant risks. Applying Birch v. UCL Hospital a 1% risk is an objectively significant risk particularly measured against the potential harm, therefore D would have a duty of care to warn and failed to do so as it is objectively significant. See also Chester v. Afshar. Also important is that there is only a duty for elective surgery LT v. Lothian NHS Health Board. 

Montgomery v. Lanarkshire Health Board -> SC established that the test is the reasonable pateitn test, rather than Bolam 'peer professional opinion,' test. The question is would a reasonable person in the patient's position be likely to attach significance to the risk. 

Webster v. Burton Hosps NHS Foundation Trust -> CA confirmed that 'the assesment of whether a risk is material cannot be reduced to percentages,' so while likeleihood can be a starting point it is also to be weighed against the severity of the harm in question. 

Birch v. UCL and Chester v. Afshar were serious injuries, death in Birch, and paralysis in Afshar, so facial paralysis at 1% probably meets the reasonable patient test, being objectively significant and therefore there was a duty to warn, and a breach of that duty in failure to warn.

In terms of the injury that was sustained, it was the inverse, very unlikely and not very significant, therefore unlikely to be objectively significant. It could still be subjectively significant. 

Australian Case Rodgers v. Whittiker -> Australian so advisory but very low likeleihood of blindness spreading from one eye to the other after an operation, found that it was subjectively significant and therefore that low risk should have been explained to the patient. However in that case the harm was much greater since C was able to live a full life with one eye, an ability she had taken from her by the operation even if the chance of that occurance happening was extremely low. However there is little evidence that injury to salival glands was all that important to Wayne, there is no indication that he was more vulnerable from it, if it had been said that the damage to his saliva glands had made it harder for him to work then maybe, but still unlikely, but to him fixing his jaw was the most important thing. 

WAYNE v. GREGORY -> Tort of assault, covered later 'trespass to the person,' covered in a separate tort.

WAYNE v. NHS TRUST -> Pure ommission for act done by a third party. Duty owed to prevent a third party. Two cases of relevance would be Home Office v. Dorset Yacht co. and Parma v. Tees. In dorset D had total control over the third party at fault, and therefore a duty could be made out because of that level of control over the third party. Contrast with Parma where the control was much less, a year had passed between contact with the NHS psychiatric hospital, and that distance made the NHS not liable in that case. The question is, would 2 months be a sufficient length of time to demonstrate lack of control. I fear it would be so.
