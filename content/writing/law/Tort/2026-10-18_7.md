The Adams family visited the Green Golf Country Club one weekend, and suffered a series of mishaps. Pauline was not interested in golf, and decided to visit a friend who lived adjacent to the Country Club. While sitting in the greenhouse, Pauline was hit in the face by a golf ball which had sailed over from the Country Club and crashed into the greenhouse, also showering Pauline in small glass fragments. Figures showed that golf balls were hit out off the club’s golf-course at least once a month, but this was the first such injury notified to the club.

Pauline v. Country Club 

Bolton v. Stone is the operative case here, Bolton v. Stone, hit by a cricket ball, matches had been played there since 1864 and the year at the time was 1951, The ball had flew through the air and gotten that far 6 times in 38 years. 

There is a 4 quadrant analysis, so the magnitude or probaiblity of harm,, what was the probaibility of harm being suffered if steps were not taken. Once a month means high probability, but there was no reporting. The question becomes was it reasonable for the golf course to know about the harm being done, if they knew or could have reasonably known the high risk of harm then that would be sufficient. 

Stimpson v. Wolverhampton MBC -> It was a regular occurance that golf balls were hit according to the testimony of a park attendant. So the figures and how they were compiled matters here. 

Given that the greenhouse might have stood for such a long time with no such incident, or that it was exceptional for the balls to go in this particular direction could defeat it. 

Important to avoid Roe, applying new eyes to the past situation. 

Generally the frequency would be high which points towards a finding that it was below the standard of care. 

The gravity/seriousness. Being hit in the face with a golf ball is a severe injury and that was found in Bolton too. So this element would be met. 

Next would be the cost and practicality of precuations, this would depend on the factual matrix of the situation, in the case of Bolton the costs of putting up a fence would be prohibitive, but the standard for a golf ball is a net of some sort, it would depend on the cost though balanced against the other factors could be justified. 

Social value/utility considerations

In Bolton this was a big deal as building an expensive fence would prevent the club for operating and totally defeat the point of the club existing. This could also be found but would have to be balanced against the other elements of the quadrant. 

Likely breach of duty found against the country club. Directly liable for their conduct. 

Pauline required immediate emergency surgical treatment. This was undertaken by Dr Sharla, a newly-qualified and relatively-inexperienced cosmetic surgeon. Unfortunately, a very small surgical sponge was left in Pauline’s sinus cavity, which caused her great discomfort, until it was discovered and removed 18 months later. Dr Sharla adduced no evidence at trial as to how the sponge came to be left there. Unaware of the drama, Pauline’s father, Ben, took a round of golf. At one point on the course, he was overcome with fumes and collapsed unconscious. 

Pauline v. Dr. Sharla 

Inexperience does not lower the standard of care but the standard of care is not perfection merely that of a reasonable doctor in this position. Would a reasonable doctor have made the same mistake. She is a surgeon so the standard would also be higher for expertise.

Ultimately likely that breach could be found. 

Investigations revealed that the owners of the Country Club had decided to irrigate the course with a new liquid fertiliser treatment, and this liquid fertiliser was kept in a shed on the course, where it was stored in a number of metal drums. The drums gave off fumes and vapours, because (according to the scientific evidence), the metal of the drums had become corroded by the contact with the liquid fertiliser, although this reaction was not scientifically known when the incident happened. The medical evidence also shows that the vapour was usually harmless to humans, but it affected Ben only because he had weak bronchial tubes.

Ben v. Country Club 

Roe, do not look at the facts of the case with new eyes, given that the side effect of corrosion was not known at the time then the conduct would not have breached the duty. 

The relevance to Ben's disability is not that relevant but presupposing the reaction was known at the time, applying Henley the blind disability case then breach could be found in that case. 

Meanwhile, Ben’s wife, Rebecca, decided to pamper herself at the Country Club’s beauty salon, and to have a pedicure. During the pedicure undertaken by Carmel, an assistant working in the salon dropped some plates accidentally whilst making Rebecca a cup of tea. Hearing the loud crash of crockery, Carmel momentarily took her eyes off Rebecca’s foot, cutting it accidentally with her sharp instrument. Carmel gave Rebecca a band-aid for the cut, but it became infected.

Rebecca v. Carmel (Vicarious/Direct liability for Country club)

Phillips v. William Whitley is the operative case here, where it was held that the standard is that of a peer professional so in this case a cosmetic professional. Given that this was a sudden freak event and also an accident then I do not believe the standard to have been breached.

Have any of these parties committed a breach of duty? Explain, by reference to relevant authorities and/or statute.

Breach has multiple elements which may raise or lower the expected standard of care. A breach occurs when the conduct in question falls below the standard of care line and that is the central element, but what constitutes reasonable care will depend on the factual matrix of a given situation. 

Roe v. Ministry of Health -> The carbonic acid with the anaesthetic injections, where the lack of colouration in the acid meant it could contaminate through micro fractures in the glass that were not known of at the time. So therefore because unknown at the time it could not have been a breach. 

Bolton v. Stone the standard of care is the standard of an ordinarily careful man or an ordinarily careful cricket club. The standard is not perfection. 

Hamilton v. Papkura DC -> The law does not impose an unattainable standard that guarantees against all harm and all circumstances.' Privy council case so somewhat advisory. 

Barrie v. Cardiff CC -> Standard of care is a question of law, the playground concrete edging case, held that reasonableness is the standard and not perfection.

It is not an average standard of care, and each act is to be judged on its own rather than the whole thing. 

Wilsher v. Essex AHA -> Claimant born prematurely and went to the special baby unit, and survived without brain damage which was remarkable but suffered blindness as a result of a misplaced catheter which led to oxygen supersaturation. Found that there was a breach in the standard of care even though the rest of the care had been exemplary and it had been a really difficult case. ALSO relevant in terms of inexperience, which does not lower the standard of care. 

The main charactetristics that could alter Standard of Care are 

Specialism which increases the standard of care in line with the speciality (See Lilywhite v. University College London Hospital NHS trust falx case)

Age which lowers the standard of care and also the situations where a duty could be found too. 

Disability where known is equal.

Disability where it is unknown is lower. 

Inexperience has no impact 

Parents have a higher standard of care

Gender has no impact

Agony of the moment/battle conditions lower it

EXPERTISE

The principal case for specialism is Lilywhite where a professor who was very experienced at dealing with these scans and he had such a high level of skill. Shadows mimicry but the high level of specialism meant the standard of care is raised.

Compare with

Holt v. Edge where a patient presented to a GP with atypical symptoms of a brain hemorrage. Held that the standard of care was met because a GP is not the same as a specialist and it goes back to this reasonable standard. The standard of an ordinary GP

Shakur v. Situ -> The case related to Chinese hurbal medicine, there was no breach because D had met the standard for a reasonable practicioner of Chinese herbal medicine. 

Phillips v. William Whitely ltd. -> D a jeweler who pierced ears did the industry standard of passing instruments through a flame, rather than antisceptic solution. held that he had met the standard for a jeweler at a retail so no breach. 

Darnley v. Croydon Health Services NHS -> Man hit his head while drunk on a night out, and went to A and E, told that he wouldn't be seen for hours, but he would be triaged in about half an hour to an hour. Breach was proven and the standard was an averagely competent and well informed person performing the fucntion of a receptionist at a department providing emergency medical care. 

AGE

Carthmartenshire CC v. Lewis -> SoC will be reduced for a child. Case about the kindergarden and the kid running into the road. 

Alice v. Kelly -> 8 Year old child "uncommon," that a child of that age could owe a duty of care but 12 high enough. Old enough but what is reasonable will change based on the age. 

Mullen v. Richards -> Teacher at a school where 15 year olds are mucking around with their erasers. The ruler broke and the eraser went into a friend's right eye. CA hheld that there was no breach . Ordinary reasonable prudent 15 year old is the standard, so like for like based on the person concerned. 

DISABILITY UNKNOWN

Mansfield v. Weetabix -> Driver for Weetabix who entered a fuge state from hypoglycemia, which led to him crashing into a shop. He did not know of his condition and that lowered the standard of care significantly to a degree that he could not be held liable. 

DISABILITY KNOWN

C v. Burcombe -> Pateitn with a heart condition and knew about it. Told not to take strenouous activity, but he was driving opted to change tire by himself, had a heart attack, veered into the next lane and crashed killing himself and others. Held that the standard of care was unchanged by the disability because of the prior knowledge.

INEXPERIENCE 

Nettleship v. Weston -> Driving student who drove into a telegraph pole on her third lesson. Teahcer checked to see if she had insurance and she did before doing the lessons. Held CA 2:1 that the standard was that of a reasonable driver. 

Wilshire v. Essex AHA -> Another case related to inexperience with the super saturated blood oxygen in the premature baby leading to permanent blindness. Held that the standard was unchanged due to inexperience.
