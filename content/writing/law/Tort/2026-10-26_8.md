One day, Adam (who had only just acquired his driving license) was driving Betty to a medical appointment when they were involved in a car accident. Adam made a right-hand turn in front of oncoming traffic when there was insufficient time to do so. Carl slammed into Adam’s car, and the collision between the two vehicles spun both around violently. Carl died from injuries sustained in the accident.


Carl v. Adam

Duty -> Robinson Recognized category, Nevilleship driver to road user. A duty of care exists between Adam and Carl. Fatal Accidents Act (action brought by estate). Though this would rely on Carl having dependents, no dependents means no cause of action under FAA.

Breach -> This was not an unavoidable accident, Barrie v. Cardiff cc. it is reasonable but not perfect. However a road traffic accident is held to be unreasonable and rightly so. Inexperience does not impact anything, Nevilleship rule, therefore the standard of care that A would be held to would be that of a reasonable driver, and that was breached by getting into an accident. It was also foreseeable that driving into oncoming traffic was going to lead to an accident. There was some harm that arose. 

Betty v. Adam

Duty -> Robinson Recognized category, Nevilleship driver to road user. A duty of care exists between Adam and Carl. Not impacted by statute as only the mother has immunity and even then only when not driving. Though Pearson debates about reform.

Breach -> Nevilleship rule again. Inexperience counts for nothing.

Therefore Adam has liability for duty and breach in the tort of negligence for both Betty and Carl's injuries. 


After the collision, Betty immediately felt a very strong abdominal pain and she was rushed to hospital. She underwent immediate scans which showed that the foetus was suffering from acute distress, and the baby (named Deidre) was born prematurely. Deidre survived, but suffered from serious physical disabilities which are attributed to the injuries which she suffered as a consequence of the car accident. Betty suffered a broken arm too.

Betty (on behalf of Deidre) v. Adam

Duty -> Recognized category driver road user, Robinson, Nevilleship. CDA 1976 at issue, but there is a duty that exists between Betty and the hospital and given that the baby was born alive, then Betty could bring a claim. 

Breach -> Established previously

Betty (on behalf of Deidre) v. Hospital

Duty -> Dr Patient recognized category, Robinson, Lillywhite.

Breach -> No indication of conduct which lead to harm and therefore likely no need to consider breach.

Adam also faces liability for the injuries to Deidre.

Adam was particularly distraught over the injuries to Betty and Deidre. He was administered a strong sedative, Xalta, by Dr Low whilst at the hospital. Thereafter, Adam’s breathing became laboured, and he turned blue. Tests showed that he suffered from hypoxic shock as a result of the medication. In fact, Xalta was not recommended for men who were also on blood-thinners (as Adam was), but Dr Low was not aware of that prohibition and had not read the pharmaceutical warnings. The sedative Relax was much more commonly-prescribed, and had no equivalent contra-indications for those who were taking blood-thinners.


Adam v. Dr. Low (vicarious against hospital or direct against hospital)

Duty -> Dr Patient recognized category, Robinson Lilywhite. 

Breach -> No act of God, Setting the standard, Dr. is a specialism and so the standard of care would be raised compare Lilywhite against Holt, a doctor is not a consultant so it would not be extremely raised, but it is higher than a GP, it's reasonable to say that it would be raised. However given the accident there could be battlefield conditions, though distraught is not a life threatening situation and therefore battlefield conditions unlikely. Bolam test applied Dr. Fielding stating that they were interchangeable is not a whole peer body and applying the Bolitho gloss, then it may be unreasonable to conclude that they are interchangeable given that blood thinners are fairly commonly prescribed and that the interaction can be dangerous. It is illogical that Dr. Low acted to give the less commonly prescribed medication and did not read the label.

Therefore Dr. Low likely faces liability against Adam.

Assume that 

(1) Dr Low concedes that, if he was in breach (which he denies), Xalta caused Adam’s hypoxic shock; and 

(2) Dr Low’s expert witness Dr Fielding will state that Xalta and Relax were ‘inter- changeable in a hospital setting’.
