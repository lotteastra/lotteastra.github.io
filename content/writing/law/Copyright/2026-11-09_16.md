Infringement, two types, primary and secondary.

PRIMARY INFRINGEMENT

1. D carried out an activity that falls within the copyright owner's control

2. D's work was derviced from the copyrighted work (causal connection)

3. The restricted act was carried out in relation to the work or a substantial part of it.

Restricted activities are in relation to the nature of the rights and therefore connected to the subject matter.

'Causal connection,' 

D's material is derived from C's work. Need to show a causal link. 

CDPA S.16(3)(b) -> The copying doesn't need to be from the original work. It does not matter if the reproduction is legitimate or pirated. 

O. Bracha, ‘The Work of Copyright in the Age of Machine Production’ -> Scholarly argument outputs of an AI system might infringe copyright in material on which the model was trained even though the trained model itself is not an infringing copy.

If D's material was derived fro copyright work is a matter of fact. C must persuade that this has occured. (See BBC v. Mitchell, no infringement where D had generated own betting odds and then later referred to those of C leaving theirs unaltered, but if they had altered their odds then that would have been derviative.)

SAS Institute v. World programming -> D may admit that they drew on C's work, or a third party might've witnessed the apropriation or an ex-employee might give evidence that their employer asked them to copy. 

John Kaldor Fabricmaker UK v. Lee Ann Fashions -> AT para 11 'spies who were at the defendant's elbow during the relevant time are seldom avaiable.' Usually the courts have to figure it out themselves.

IPC Media v. Highbury Leisure -> Courts have been willing to infer derivation.

Billhofer Maschinenfabrik GmbH v. Dixon & co. -> 'Resemblences of inessentials, the small, redudnant or even mistaken elements of the copyright work shall carry the most weight,'

Ibcos Computers v. Barclays Merchantile Highland Finance -> When the order in which a list of variables appears was a matter of arbitrary choice then the reappearance of that order in later code is certainly a powerful indication of copying.

Designers GUild v. Russell  -> C created a fabric design Ixia, made of pink and yellow stripes with flowers. The fabric was in shops from 1995, a year later they found D was selling fabric with a design called Marguerite, also based on vertical shapes in alternating colours with flowers and associated stalks and leaves scattered across the stripes. 

There were differences. Asserting the designer in Russell had used a cherry blossom design. However it was found in the CA and HoL that it had been copied. Both designs were based on stripes with scattered flowers in an impressionistic style with brushwork in both. The judge concluded similarities went far beyond the similiarities that could be expected from a given situation. The designer also saw the other design at a trade show. 

BUT!

Mitchell v. BBC -> Claim that D had copied C's character designs. The bouonce bunch were a group of strange looking robots. Although there were sufficient similarities to make the BBC prove independent creation, there was evidence that could be produced. 

Francis Day & Hunter v. Bron -> Equally in the case of subconscious copying the courts may be willing to make such a finding.

CDPA S.16(3) taking a substantnial part of a work is also infringement.

There's a conflict with EU law where it is a part of a work. However since part of a work is part of AOIC then since we follow that we will continue to use a part. 

However this is in relation to original works.

Unclear if the old substantial part applies for other subject matter and it won't apply for the unharmonized published editions and typographical arrangements. 

Partial copying of authorial works

Infopaq -> 11 words was sufficient to find infringement in that case. 'The various parts of a work enjoy protection provided that they contain elements which are the expression of the intellectual creation of the author of the work.

Football Association v. QC Leisure -> Reiterated this approach. Infringement of copyright in works contained in films that have been broadcast by satelite. 

SAS Institute v. World Programming -> Question of whether parts of SAS's computer manuals reproduced were substantial as requriing analsysi of whether the sections that were reproduced were original. 

"In Infopaq the court said that parts of a work are entitled to the same protection as the work as a whole. But the parts in question must ‘contain elements which are the expression of the intellectual creation of the author of the work" SAS at para 39

"This is now the test for determining whether a restricted
act has been done in relation to a substantial part of a work"

There are five main features

1. Protection depends on the originality of the part taken.

A person will infringe only if they appropriate a part of C's work that is original. (See Designer's Guild)

Williamson Music v. Pearson -> D had created a parody of C's lyrics, the only lyrics that were copied was the repetition of 'we got,' and the words were not substantial enough to constitute infringement. At 107

Though! Taking repeatedly these non-original elements may be infringing.

2. The irrelevance of D's creativity

Translations are still held to meet this even thoguh there is a lot of creativity involved in making them

Designer's guild is a good example of D's creativity being 'irrelevant.' 

3. Audience Perception

What a given audience thinks

4. Dematerialization

Infopaq asks whether what is taken is itself original. J. Griffiths, ‘Dematerialization, Pragmatism and the European Copyright Revolution’ no need to worry about the perameters of the work being infringed.

5. Works of minimal creativity. 

Good case is Lidl v. Tesco -> The elements are quite narrow and as such it would need more similarity in order to be infringement in the first place.

Same as in 

Courtenay Smith & Anor v. THe NOtting Hill Shopping Bag Company ltd & ors -> IPEC found that there was copyright in the logo on the bag but it was not infringed because the specific original elements were small. 

6. Intent

Intention has been a past thing and now we just focus on AOIC.

7. Repeated Takings

In the past there has been a question about the amount needed to constitute infringement. 

Newspaper Licensing Agency v. Marks & SPencer -> Systemic copying could be infringement in that case. 

!Non-Literal Copying

Copyright does not just protect the specific thing being produced ie. the specific words in specific order. 

The elements may include the plot, storyline and even characters following the del boy case. Those would be protected.

Same for computer programs. Particular characteristics of speed, efficiency and style. 

Musical works it may be the melody phrasing and rhythmn. (See Coffey v. Warner/Chappell)

Krisarts SA v. Briarfine -> D commissioned artist G to paint scenes including Parliament and Westminster Bridge. D showed G postcards of the scenes, and prints from another artist L. G's paintings were made from the same view and angle as L. Whitford J stressed that other painters should not be prevented from painting the same scenes he did say that some elements were distinctive enough to warrant being protected. 

!Non-protection of ideas

Oren Bracha characterizes these as ‘spillovers’ O. Bracha, ‘The Work of Copyright in the Age of Machine Production’ (2024) 38 Harv JL& Tech 171. -> Third parties can do whatever they want with the ideas that inform a copyrighted work. 

Designers Guild v. Williams 2000 1 WLR 2416, 2423 (Lord Hoffmann) -> Some debate as to if non-protection of ideas is more built around the fact that copyright just protects the expression rather than anything else. 

Mio and Konektra, Cases C-580/23 and C-795/23, ECLI EU C 2025 941, 74 (post-Brexit). ->  ‘the fundamental principle of copyright’.

However there's a fine line between the idea and the exression with expression being the development of ideas. 

Jones v. London Borough of Tower Hamlets 2001 RPC (23) 407, 418–19 -> Ideas go unprotected because it would be contrary to the public interest

Kleeneze v. DRG (UK) 1984 FSR 399. -> Highlighting the centrality of the idea–expression dichotomy to the policy of avoiding the protection of basic concepts for clothing designs.

Cofomel at para 48 -> 'the competitors of the author of a computer program are free,
once they establish through independent analysis which ideas, rules or principles are being used, to create their own implementation of them in order to create compatible products. They may, moreover, build on the identical idea’

!The Subject of a Work

Kenrick v. Lawrence -> A case where a hand pointing illiterate voters where to vote while that was protected by copyright the work was not so original that a hand pointing in the same context would be and it would have to be exact recreation commenserate to the level of originality in the original work. 

‘World Cup Willie’ case -> C's work was a lion playing football, and though D had copied the idea of a lion kicking a ball, the postures were different and so were the angles of the faces and the level of stylization in both cases. The court therefore concluded that the ideas were the same but the expression was different. Not a substantial part of the original. 

Temple Island Collections v. New English Teas -> Ideas was narrow when finding infringement of a photograph of a red double decker bus crossing westminster bridge, with a white background, infringement was found where the background was black in the second case. There was cricicism since the idea was a fairly common one in that case. 

Mei Fields Designs v. Saffron Cards and gifts -> Designing greeting cards using fabric, beads and stylized words and D had distributed their goods with C. The court found some of the cards to be infringements but most of them were infringement of ideas. So two cakes with wedding day was infringement of the expression but a where the subject depicted was the same, a heart was an example, but the style was quite different then there was no infringement for those cards. 

!Basic Components

Sheeran v. Chokri -> Building blocks of works cannot be copyrightable at para 206

'I accept the claimants’ contention that none of the elements of similarity on which the defendants rely in itself represents that which confers originality on Oh Why as a musical work. The use of the rising minor pentatonic scale is a generic and commonplace building block in many musical genres. The fact that each note of the scale is repeated does not in my judgment alter that conclusion. The use of a vocal chant and its harmonisation with low and high octaves are equally generic and commonplace ideas.'

Cofomel -> Copyright in the designs of jeans and shirts, the specific composition of colours words and numbers, combination of colours and putting a pocket on the stomach would be to basic to be infringing. 

!Facts and theories 

Baigent v. Random House -> Da Vinci Code did not infringe 'The Holy Blood and the Holy Grail,' C argued that the historical work posessed a central theme, with 15 elements and theories about Christ, but while the DaVinci code did follow 11/15 the ideas were too abrstract for infringing to be possible. 

Hodgson v. Isaac -> Film script of a wheelchair bound football fan was infringing a biography since the parties had been in talks to create an actual adaptation but the consent was withdrawn. Striking details and similar interpretations was infringing. 

!Style

IPC Media -> Publishers of a magazine brought proceedings against another competitor claiming that the covers of the competitor's magazine infringed their own covers. Laddie J explained however that 

"The law of copyright has never gone as far as to protect general themes, styles or ideas. Monet, like those before him, acquired no right to prevent others from painting flowers or even water lilies or, … Georges Seurat would not have obtained, through copyright, the right to prevent others from painting in a pointillist style. Even someone who is inspired by Monet to paint water lilies or by Seurat to paint using coloured dots would not infringe copyright. Such general concepts are not put out of bounds to others by the law of copyright."

Therefore there was no infringement.

Norozian v. Arks -> Found a similar point in that case that 

"If, on seeing La Baignade, Asnières at the Salon des Artistes Indépendants in 1884, another artist had used precisely the same technique in painting a scene in Provence, Seurat would have been unable, by the canons of English copyright law, to maintain an action against him. Other examples of original artistic styles or techniques whose imitation in the production of an entirely different subject matter would not found such an action might be the ‘sprung rhythm’ of Gerard Manley Hopkins’ verse or the thematic build-up of Sibelius’s second symphony."

Shantell Martin -> Something more than just style has to be copied in order to constitute infringement. Shantell Martin drew in black and white and then made human faces out of these landscape maps drawn in black and white. D imported wine from Argentina with labels that were said to be infringing these designs. The ones that featured faces made from line drawings of landscapes were infringing, but not the ones that were just of landscapes. 

Exclusion of style from copyright is under pressure from AI where models can generate outputs in the style of someone known. (O. Bracha, ‘The Work of Copyright in the Age of Machine Production’ (2024) 38 Harv JL & Tech 171, 211–13.
)

!Exclusion of functional expression

Copyright law does not protect functionality which is patent law. 

Designers Guild v. Williams -> Some ideas are not protected because 'they have no connection witht eh LDMA nature of the work.' 

Kleenze v. DRG -> No infringement of copyright when C drew a letterbox draught excluder and D went on to make that excluder. 

SAS Institute v. Wrodl Programming -> D had developed a program that emulated the functoining of C's set of programs that was used in statiscial analysis. No suggestion that D had access to the source code, question arose if it had infringed the computer program or the manuals, Arnold J observed that copyright protects the expression and not 'ideas, proceedures, methods of opereation and mathematical concepts as such,' 

UK justice found no infringement as the functionality were unprocteted but referred the matter to the EU.

CJEU agreed that they were not protected as a computer program and that the functionality of the computer program was also not protectable as it would be. ‘possible to monopolise ideas, to the detriment of technological progress and industrial development.' 

Navitaire v. Easyjet and Nova Productions v. Mazooma Games -> Navitaire, copyright owner in source code brought action against a former licensee who didn't see the code but tried to emulate the program, the Jusitce found no infringement since the functional behaviour of a program was different from the plot of a novel. 

Nova Productions -> Similarities between video games were attributable to general ideas that didn't have much to do with the skill and effort of the gprogrammer. The CA found that what was found to have inspired some aspects of D's game was too general to make any part of C's game. 

Post Brexit CJEU indicated that supply of software that allowed a user to alter the operation of a sony game but did so through modifying the value of variable in the RAM did not implicate the expression of the computer program. 

!Entreprenurial Works and Related Rights

Protection is limited to the form in which the work is fixed. Signal Copyrights, following Arnold LJ

Pelham GmbH v. Ralf Hutter -> Short audio snatches sampling from usiic were used in another song and the German court referred the question of what 'part,' of a signal was to the CJEU. The CJEU held that any part no matter how short was infringement. However this was the EU standard the UK now we've left might be different. 

England and Wales Cricket Board and Sky UK v. Tixdaq and Fanatix -> Court was assessing the legitimacy of a mobile platform that allowed users to broadcast smalll snippets of cirkcet broadcast footgage. 

'Parts of broadcasts and first fixations of films enjoy protection … provided that they contain elements which reflect the rationale for protecting broadcasts and first fixations, that is to say, the investment made by the broadcaster or producer. I do not consider that … reproduction of any part of a broadcast or first fixation amounts to an infringement … the correct test of substantiality is to consider the degree of reproduction both quantitatively and qualitatively, having regard to the extent to which the reproduction exploits the investment made by the broadcaster or producer' Arnold J in that case. 

Found that highlights was copyright of the parts of the work and some of the most significant parts. Exploited the investment was the rationale in that. 

Football Association Premier League v. QC Leisure -> Found that by contrast four frames of a video stream held in a decoder was not a substantial part because it was for just a fraction of a second with no inherent value. 

Pelham v. Huetter -> Interpreted any part, they then considered the test applicable for distribution of sound recordings and used a different standard for distribution of copy than for reproduction of a part. Infringing the distribution right of a recording would have had to reproduce the whole or substantial part of the recording. 

!Typographical Arrangements 

Newspaper Licensing Agency v. Marks and Spencer -> HoL held that each edition of the newspaper rather than each article benefited from typographical copyright. Threshold set fairly high for reproduction in that case.

SECONDARY INFRINGEMENT

Secondary infringement would be commercial context dealing with infringing copies of an infringing public performance. This has two elements. Mental and acts.

CDPA S.22 and 23 are the sections which deal with secondary infringement. S.27(2)

AI Training 

Getty Images v. Stability AI -> Court considered if models of stable diffusion that could be downloaded were imported despite being trained in the US. The court held that models would constitute articles but there was no liability because there weren't any infringing copies. S.17 envisaged electronic copying, the legislature swould not have intended that to be limited to just corporeal elements. However Getty did not argue that its images were copied into the trained model, more that training would have been infringement if it had happened in the UK. However because the model was not the work, it was not an infringing copy. The article needs to consist or store a copy. The decision is being appealed though.
