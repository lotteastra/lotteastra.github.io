Infringement, two types, primary and secondary.

PRIMARY INFRINGEMENT

1. D carried out an activity that falls within the copyright owner's control

2. D's work was derviced from the copyrighted work (causal connection)

3. The restricted act was carried out in relation to the work or a substantial part of it.

Restricted activities are in relation to the nature of the rights and therefore connected to the subject matter.

'Causal connection,' 

D's material is derived from C's work. Need to show a causal link. 

CDPA S.16(3)(b) -> The copying doesn't need to be from the original work. It does not matter if the reproduction is legitimate or pirated. 

O. Bracha, ‘The Work of Copyright in the Age of Machine Production’ -> Scholarly argument outputs of an AI system might infringe copyright in material on which the model was trained even though the trained model itself is not an infringing copy.

If D's material was derived fro copyright work is a matter of fact. C must persuade that this has occured. (See BBC v. Mitchell, no infringement where D had generated own betting odds and then later referred to those of C leaving theirs unaltered, but if they had altered their odds then that would have been derviative.)

SAS Institute v. World programming -> D may admit that they drew on C's work, or a third party might've witnessed the apropriation or an ex-employee might give evidence that their employer asked them to copy. 

John Kaldor Fabricmaker UK v. Lee Ann Fashions -> AT para 11 'spies who were at the defendant's elbow during the relevant time are seldom avaiable.' Usually the courts have to figure it out themselves.

IPC Media v. Highbury Leisure -> Courts have been willing to infer derivation.

Billhofer Maschinenfabrik GmbH v. Dixon & co. -> 'Resemblences of inessentials, the small, redudnant or even mistaken elements of the copyright work shall carry the most weight,'

Ibcos Computers v. Barclays Merchantile Highland Finance -> When the order in which a list of variables appears was a matter of arbitrary choice then the reappearance of that order in later code is certainly a powerful indication of copying.

Designers GUild v. Russell  -> C created a fabric design Ixia, made of pink and yellow stripes with flowers. The fabric was in shops from 1995, a year later they found D was selling fabric with a design called Marguerite, also based on vertical shapes in alternating colours with flowers and associated stalks and leaves scattered across the stripes. 

There were differences. Asserting the designer in Russell had used a cherry blossom design. However it was found in the CA and HoL that it had been copied. Both designs were based on stripes with scattered flowers in an impressionistic style with brushwork in both. The judge concluded similarities went far beyond the similiarities that could be expected from a given situation. The designer also saw the other design at a trade show. 

BUT!

Mitchell v. BBC -> Claim that D had copied C's character designs. The bouonce bunch were a group of strange looking robots. Although there were sufficient similarities to make the BBC prove independent creation, there was evidence that could be produced. 

Francis Day & Hunter v. Bron -> Equally in the case of subconscious copying the courts may be willing to make such a finding.

CDPA S.16(3) taking a substantnial part of a work is also infringement.

There's a conflict with EU law where it is a part of a work. However since part of a work is part of AOIC then since we follow that we will continue to use a part. 

However this is in relation to original works.

Unclear if the old substantial part applies for other subject matter and it won't apply for the unharmonized published editions and typographical arrangements. 

Partial copying of authorial works

Infopaq -> 11 words was sufficient to find infringement in that case. 'The various parts of a work enjoy protection provided that they contain elements which are the expression of the intellectual creation of the author of the work.

Football Association v. QC Leisure -> Reiterated this approach. Infringement of copyright in works contained in films that have been broadcast by satelite. 

SAS Institute v. World Programming -> Question of whether parts of SAS's computer manuals reproduced were substantial as requriing analsysi of whether the sections that were reproduced were original. 

"In Infopaq the court said that parts of a work are entitled to the same protection as the work as a whole. But the parts in question must ‘contain elements which are the expression of the intellectual creation of the author of the work" SAS at para 39

"This is now the test for determining whether a restricted
act has been done in relation to a substantial part of a work"

There are five main features

1. Protection depends on the originality of the part taken.

A person will infringe only if they appropriate a part of C's work that is original. (See Designer's Guild)

Williamson Music v. Pearson -> D had created a parody of C's lyrics, the only lyrics that were copied was the repetition of 'we got,' and the words were not substantial enough to constitute infringement. At 107

Though! Taking repeatedly these non-original elements may be infringing.

2. The irrelevance of D's creativity

Translations are still held to meet this even thoguh there is a lot of creativity involved in making them

Designer's guild is a good example of D's creativity being 'irrelevant.' 

3. Audience Perception

What a given audience thinks

4. Dematerialization

Infopaq asks whether what is taken is itself original. J. Griffiths, ‘Dematerialization, Pragmatism and the European Copyright Revolution’ no need to worry about the perameters of the work being infringed.

5. Works of minimal creativity. 

Good case is Lidl v. Tesco -> The elements are quite narrow and as such it would need more similarity in order to be infringement in the first place.

Same as in 

Courtenay Smith & Anor v. THe NOtting Hill Shopping Bag Company ltd & ors -> IPEC found that there was copyright in the logo on the bag but it was not infringed because the specific original elements were small. 

6. Intent

Intention has been a past thing and now we just focus on AOIC.

7. Repeated Takings

In the past there has been a question about the amount needed to constitute infringement. 

Newspaper Licensing Agency v. Marks & SPencer -> Systemic copying could be infringement in that case. 

!Non-Literal Copying

Copyright does not just protect the specific thing being produced ie. the specific words in specific order. 

The elements may include the plot, storyline and even characters following the del boy case. Those would be protected.

Same for computer programs. Particular characteristics of speed, efficiency and style. 

Musical works it may be the melody phrasing and rhythmn. (See Coffey v. Warner/Chappell)

Krisarts SA v. Briarfine -> D commissioned artist G to paint scenes including Parliament and Westminster Bridge. D showed G postcards of the scenes, and prints from another artist L. G's paintings were made from the same view and angle as L. Whitford J stressed that other painters should not be prevented from painting the same scenes he did say that some elements were distinctive enough to warrant being protected.
