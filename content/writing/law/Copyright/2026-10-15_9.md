1.	How is the concept of “sculpture” to be interpreted under the CDPA 1988?

2.	Applying this approach, would the following be protected as “sculptures” under the CDPA 1988 if they were created in the UK for the first time today?

Breville Europe v. Thorn EMI Falconer J found that scallop shape moulds that were used to make toasted sandwiches in a machine were indeed sculptures. Early on sculptures and engravings took the same path, emphasizing how an object is made more than anything else. 

However! Later cases disagree.

Laddie J defined a sculpture as a 'three dimensional work made by an artist's hand.' This was affirmed later in Lucasfilm v. Ainsworth, where this second definition came to be emphasized. Mann J went on to say that 'some regard,' should be used for the ordinary english meaning of the word 'sculpture.' In particular Mann J argued that the object in question was not a sculpture 'not that it lacks artistic merit; it lacks artistic purpose.' The purpose makes a sculpture. 

Three dimensional work made by an artist's hand. Therefore applying to the facts the bed is made by an artist for aan artistic purpose and therefore it would qualify, as would the rest. The trophy is more arguable but not completely beyond the pale.

1.	How has the concept of “work of artistic craftsmanship” been interpreted under the CDPA 1988?

2.	IIn Waterrower (UK) Ltd. v. Liking Ltd [2024] EWHC 2806 (IPEC), why was the wooden rowing machine held not to be a “work of artistic craftsmanship” under the CDPA 1988?

Because it lacked an artistic purpose

"there will come a point where, in context, the distancing of the craftsman and their creativity from the creation of the object is such that they are no longer creating a work that would be considered as utilising craftsmanship." Waterrower at para 127

Difficult to reconcile with EU law however.

3.	Applying the approach endorsed in recent UK case-law, would the following be protected as works of artistic craftsmanship if created in the UK today?

rtistic quality is a bit of a conundrum. In Hensher v. Restawile Upholstery, each of the judges in that case gave differing rationales for artistic quality. Lord Reid emphasized that a person should get 'pleasure or satisfaction from contemplating it,' then that object would have artistic quality. Lord Morris said that it was a personal judgement based on experts in fieeld. Lord Kilbrandon said that the issue was if the author was concerned to make a piece of art. Lord Simon said that the craftsmanship rather than the work must be artistic, however he did also talk about the intent of the creator in what they have made. 

In Merlet v. Mothercare it was held that a babycape was not a work of artistic quality on the grounds that the creator did not intend to make something of artistic quality merely something utilitarian. Though in New Zealand the case of a knitted sweater was an artistic work because of the artistry and the eye of the beholder. The aesthetic appeal. In particular the special knitting led to more sales which demonstrated artistry in a manner of speaking. 

Another problematic aspect of artistry and craftsmanship is must they both be done by the same person, this was found to not be the case in the case of Burke v. Spicer's Dress Designs. The artistic element did not come from the same person who did the craftsmanship. In Bonz Group v. Cooke, a set of sweaters depicting dancing lambs and golfing kiwis was held to be a work of artistic craftsmanship and that was approved by Mann J in Lucasfilm, so long as there was collaboration between the craftsman and the artist. 

•	The Hermès Birkin bag 

•	Asplund’s Palais Royal table 

•	The Konektra shelving system 

For all three the works would need to clear two elements, they would need to be works of craftsmanship, and they would have to be artistic. For a work of craftsmanship care is shown that it is a craftsman at work and not a mechanical process where the craftsman might be distanced from his work, the next is that the intent in the creation seems to have been for an artistic rather than utilitarian purpose. In a sense any purpose in a design other than a utilitarian one could be construed to be creative and therefore allowing copyright to subsist as an artistic work.
