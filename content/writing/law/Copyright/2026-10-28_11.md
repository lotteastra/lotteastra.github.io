Question One

Anna graduated in film studies and is trying to establish a career as a director. In order to establish her reputation, she has made a short film on a shoe-string budget. The film, Cartref, is intended as an exploration of the influence of Swansea, her home city, on her life. It is predominantly a monochrome work and was made without actors. She shot many hours of footage of unscripted conversations between residents of Swansea and selected extracts for inclusion in the final version of the film. These extracts were combined with footage of the local land- and sea-scape against a soundtrack of Anna playing her own music on the piano. The film has a melancholy, poetic beauty and has won several awards for young film-makers. 

Anna met her best friend, Belinda, on her film studies’ course. They meet up every week for a coffee and a chat. Shortly after graduation, during one of these meetings, Belinda said: “You’re obsessed with Swansea. You should make a film about the hold that city has on you.” It was following this meeting that Anna began to think about making Cartref. At a number of subsequent meetings, Anna and Belinda discussed progress on the film. It was Belinda who suggested that Anna should include shots of the sea and also came up with the idea of using Anna’s own piano-playing as the soundtrack. On one occasion, Belinda even accompanied Anna to Swansea to discuss potential shots for the film. When Cartref was completed, Anna said to Belinda: “I’m really grateful for all of your help with the film. I’m going to take you out for dinner as a thank you.” 

Do any copyright works arise on the above facts? If they do, who will be the author and first owner of the copyright in those works? 

1. Anna's original work, what is the nature of the work? 

Film/Database

S.5B defines film. 

V. FILMS

Defined in the CDPA 1988 as 'a recording on any medium from which a moving image may be produced by any means.' S.5(1)b. This would include multimedia products as well. 

For Anna's work to be a film it would also need to meet S.153, connection to the UK, given that she studies here that means she resides here and the work does have a clear connection to the UK through its subject matter. It was also published in the UK clearing S.155

There is no fixation requirement for films or sound recordings. 

CJEU has given 4 broad elements

1. Expression

2. Author must be able to make free choices

3. Author must be able to make creative choices

4. The creator must have stamped their personality on the work their 'personal touch,' 

Endestra tapes case regular conversation may be copyrightable but that is not a given particularly if the conversations are merely functional. However the effect in this case would be that the copyright would not exist for the people giving the conversation to claim against Anna.

For Anna she did meet the AOIC threshold which applies in THJ v. Sherida. The author was expressing their feeling of a place, through free and creative choices that stamped their personality or personal touch. The second and third limb are more emphasized in UK courts and easily cleared here.

Therefore her work would likely be protected as a film. 

In terms of co-ownership. The relevant case is Kogan v. Martin, where they decided on a numerical 80-20 split based on contributions. The contributions were about the ideas of a film to a writer, and they were given no credit in the end. Ultimately it would depend on the scale of contribution and would be for the courts to decide if there was co-ownership. 


Question Two

Answer the following questions:

(1)	What is the intended purpose of CDPA, s 9(3)?

The aim was to “allow investment in artificial intelligence systems, in the future, to be made with confidence”. 

(2)	What are the problems in interpreting CDPA, s 9(3)?

135. First, there appears to be a legal contradiction within section 9(3) which leads to uncertainty about its interpretation. This is because the provision applies only to literary, dramatic, musical, and artistic works which are original. The modern legal test for originality is that a work must be an “author’s own intellectual creation” which is the expression of their creative choices and reflects their “personal touch”.[footnote 1] This test is very much associated with human qualities, suggesting that a work created by a non-human could not be “original”. However, section 9(3) only applies to works “without a human author”.

There is a direct internal contradiction with the law and that creates clear uncertainty.

(3)	How might the protection for “computer-generated works” under the CDPA apply in the case of AI outputs?

Because it protects the person for whom the generation was undertaken it would theoretically protect the prompter in the case of AI generated works.

(4)	 In response to the consultation document, do you agree with their conclusions and proposals?

I agree with the sense that S.9(3) is despite being comparatively recently passed and admitedly rather forward thinking, perhaps even possibly fit for purpose in 1988, and until 2022 the same might have been said, it is now outdated. The central condtradiction substantially weakened it and in relation to AI works there's no protection afforded to the data that may have been infringed by the AI company without credit or compensation, while incentivizing production of AI work that could be copyrighted. Ultimately, I think that there needs to be clarity and a bespoke regime of infringement and enforcement for AI model training, and that the subsequent works should be owned by either the company who made the model or the public.

Arguments for the public -> If infringement is allowed then the milleu of public works means that the function of AI is to feed the public the previous old works back to them in a different order. Equally AI companies aren't selling intellectual property per se, they're selling access to the models, public domain may well be in the company's interest because it would mean more people would know about the usefulness of their tools. Equally nobody could monopolize intellectual property that they might have had a very limited role in creating.

Arguments for the model company -> It would incentivize AI companies to set up shop here, though the conferral of such a right could be monopolistic and it may not be within the AI company's interest to enforce this right as it could hamper adoption.
