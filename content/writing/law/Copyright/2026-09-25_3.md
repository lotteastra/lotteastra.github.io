title: Copyright Notes Subject Matter
---

Following the Copyright Designs and Patents Act 1988 (CDPA 1988) stipulates that there are 8 categories of subject matter that are protected. 

1. Literary works

2. Dramatic works

3. Musical works

4. Artistic works

5. Films 

6. Sound recordings

7. Broadcasts

8. Published editions 

This is a closed list and all copyrighted works must be found within this list.

I. LITERARY WORKS

Found in S3(1) of the CDPA 1988

Statutory definition 

"any work, other than  a dramatic or musical work, which is written, spoken or sung, and accordingly includes (a) a table or compliation (other than a database), (b) a computer program, (c) preparatory design material for a computer program, and (d) a database."

Writing includes symbols and numerals. All literary work is protected regardless of quality or style. 

Hollinrake v. Truswell test, a book must provide 'either information and instruction, or pleasure in the form of literary enjoyment.' It must be capable of conveying intelligable meaning. That it is capable of being made sense of is all that is necessary, therefore work encoded in a cipher would be a literary work. Gibberish however would not count. 

Names however cannot be copyrighted and are protected instead through trademark. Names by themselves do not meet the Hollinrake test. The CJEU also approved of this sort of reading in Infopaq, whereby words 'considered in isolation, are not as such an intellectual creation of the author who employs them.' There are also policy implications to this end.

Addendum i. Characters 

Shazam Productions v. Only Fools the Dining Experience, in this case a character was protected as being a literary work. However this addendum has been subject to scruitiny and the case where they were decided as being protected as a literary work was decided under the EU as such it remains to be seen if the UK courts will continue to follow in this direction.

Addendum ii. Computer Programs

Follows a 1980s agreement which saw computer programs as literary works. However this does not apply to a GUI, which gives clues that courts see a literary work as primarily something written and not something that merely conveys information through images. Chinese speakers in shambles. With regard to the EU, post Brexit so at best a persuasive authority it was also found that the code written out was protected but that alone, and hence modifications to it did not fall within infringement, see 'Sony Computer Entertainmeent Europe v. Datel Design and Development.' 

Addendum iii. Databases

Part of an EU directive and defined in statute, S.3(1)(d) CDPA 1988, 'a collection of independent works, data or other materials which are arranged in a systemtic or methodlical way, and individually accessible by electronic or other means.' 

Fixtures Marketing v. Organismos Prognostikon Agonon Podosfairou AE, the CJEU construed database widely so as to include a list of English football fixtures. 

A database also would be protected as a literary work even if it had audiovisual components. 

In the case just mentioned the definining element that makes a database is that the information is not 'seperable from one another without their informative or other value being affected,' in effect the dependence on the other information in a database is what makes a database a database. 

II. DRAMATIC WORKS

The CDPA does not define what a dramatic work is, except that it is a work of dance or mime. In Norowzian v. Arks (no. 2) the defining features are that a dramatic work is a 'work of action,' that is 'capable of being performed.' This was used in that case to say that an animated film may be a dramatic work because by the act of playing it, it may be performed and therefore would be protected as that. Though these works must be original, and therefore it would not include a recording of a sports game since that is not an original creation of the one taking the film. That case centred around the fact that an ad was mimicing the impossible performance of an animated film, and since the film was capable of being performed (read, playeed in public), it was therefore a dramatic work. 

Addendum iv. TV formats

Hughie Green case it was found that TV formats were not dramatic works, this was on account of the lack of unity between the different versions of each TV game show that was filmed, TV game shows being the subject matter in that case. The scripts only gave a 'general idea,' of the content and therefore the spontineuity defeated the finding that this was a dramatic work. 

Addendum v. Interactive Games

Found in Nova Productions v. Mazooma Games that even where two games were very similar the lackk of certainty and unity made the games incapable of being truely capable of performance and therefore not a dramatic work. 

III. MUSICAL WORKS 

A musical work is defined in S.3(1) of the CDPA 1988 as 'a work consisting of music, exclusive of any words or action intended to be sung, spoken or performed with the music.' The words of songs are literary works. 

Sawkins v. Hyperion Records, provides good guidence. Sawkins published performance directions and made a few additions to a 17th century piece of music, scores with these alterations were then recorded by Hyperion. The court held that these alterations were protected as musical works as explained in that case by Mummery LJ 'the essence of music is combining sounds for listening to. Music is not mere noise. The sound of music is intended to produce effects of some kind on the listener's emotions and intellect. The sounds may be produced by an organized performance eon instruments played from a musical score, though that is not essential for the existence of the music or of copyright in it.' 

Elements that contributed to the sound were therefore protected by copyright as musical works. 

IV. Artistic Works

Defined in S.4(1) CDPA 1988, which creates three categories to define artistic works:

(i) irrespective of artistic quality, a graphic work (including a painting, drawing, diagram, map, chart or plan, engraving, etching lithograph, woodcut or similar work), a photograph (excluding a film), a sculpture or a collage;

(ii) a work of architecture, being a building or fixed structure or a model thereof; or

(iii) a work of artistic craftsmanship

Elaboration on the First, graphic works, photographs, sculptures and collages.

Quality is not important in finding protection for it. The statute makes artistic works into a list, and the courts define what each item on that list actually means. 

Graphic works includes paintings though what is a painting is not without controversy. In the case of Merchandising Corp. v. Harpbond facial makeup on a pop star was argued to be a painting and therefore to be protected but the court held that this was not the case. Lawton LJ held in that case that painting required a surface, which the face did not. This rationale does appear to be unconvincing and could be reconsidered. 

Graphic work also includes a 'drawing,' 'diagram,' 'map,' 'chart,' or 'plan.' In Technomed v. Bluecrest Health Screening, the court held that even a simple line diagram was an artistic work and therefore protected. This case involved a heart monitor. 

Engravings are also protected and engravings has been construed surprisingly widely. Engraving being about production is linked in copyright law more to the process than anything else. In Hi-Tech Autoparts v. Towergate Two, it was held that copies of a rubber floormat could not be sold because the mats themselves were an engraving of a mould. 

In Response Clothing ltd. v. The Edinburgh Woollen Mill, Hacon J, limited the notion of graphic by saying that graphic works must follow as 'examples of a graphic work set out in the subsection are created by the author making marks on a substrate to generate an image.' Therefore graphic work could not cover a wave design for jumpers. In Aga Rangemaster, this decision was further affirmed in stating that the control panel for a cooker was not a graphic work. Though this category does include a design in powder, computer screen icons and GUIs that we saw earlier.

Photographs are also protected but they are a bit difficult to fit into copyright law, because they do photograph the natural world in general. A photograph of the same arrangement by a different photographer would therefore be owned by them, rather than the original photographer, and there would be two different angles, as was the case in the Oasis case. 

Sculptures are another area of some confusion. In Breville Europe v. Thorn EMI Falconer J found that scallop shape moulds that were used to make toasted sandwiches in a machine were indeed sculptures. Laddie J defined a sculpture as a 'three dimensional work made by an artist's hand.' This was affirmed later in Lucasfilm v. Ainsworth, where this second definition came to be emphasized. Mann J went on to say that 'some regard,' should be used for the ordinary english meaning of the word 'sculpture.' In particular Mann J argued that the object in question was not a sculpture 'not that it lacks artistic merit; it lacks artistic purpose.' The purpose makes a sculpture. 

Works of Architecture. This means a building, any fixed structure. The emphasis is fixed structure which was intended to prevent ships from being copyrightable as works of architecture, though this threw up issues for greenhouses and portable cabins. 

Works of Artistic craftsmanship are the last category. This one is the most confused and arose from the 1911 copyright act. Caselaw views artistic craftsmanship as being to ideas, that there must be artistry and then craftsmanship in order to artistic craftsmanshipi make. Craftsmanship is therefore limited to works of craftspeople, think artisinal stuff, pottery, glass-blowing, iron forging, the return to nature sort of stuff. Though these activities are largely handmade this is not necessarily a requirement. In Hensher v. Restawile a work of craftsmanship is one where 'special training, skill and knowledge,' is required for its production. This test was re-enforced in Bonz v. Cooke. 

Artistic quality is a bit of a conundrum. In Hensher v. Restawile Upholstery, each of the judges in that case gave differing rationales for artistic quality. Lord Reid emphasized that a person should get 'pleasure or satisfaction from contemplating it,' then that object would have artistic quality. Lord Morris said that it was a personal judgement based on experts in fieeld. Lord Kilbrandon said that the issue was if the author was concerned to make a piece of art. Lord Simon said that the craftsmanship rather than the work must be artistic, however he did also talk about the intent of the creator in what they have made. 

In Merlet v. Mothercare it was held that a babycape was not a work of artistic quality on the grounds that the creator did not intend to make something of artistic quality merely something utilitarian. Though in New Zealand the case of a knitted sweater was an artistic work because of the artistry and the eye of the beholder. The aesthetic appeal. In particular the special knitting led to more sales which demonstrated artistry in a manner of speaking. 

Another problematic aspect of artistry and craftsmanship is must they both be done by the same person, this was found to not be the case in the case of Burke v. Spicer's Dress Designs. The artistic element did not come from the same person who did the craftsmanship. In Bonz Group v. Cooke, a set of sweaters depicting dancing lambs and golfing kiwis was held to be a work of artistic craftsmanship and that was approved by Mann J in Lucasfilm, so long as there was collaboration between the craftsman and the artist. 

Another problematic aspect of artistic craftsmanship relates to the EU which held in Cofemel, where it was held that there could be no additional criteria applied by member states. There are debates on this. Given the harmonization then a work of artistic craftsmanship can be reduced merely to works of craftsmanship with visual appeal. 

V. FILMS

Defined in the CDPA 1988 as 'a recording on any medium from which a moving image may be produced by any means.' S.5(1)b. This would include multimedia products as well. 

VI. SOUND RECORDINGS

Defined in CDPA 1988 S.5A(1) as 

(a) a recording of sounds from which the sounds may be reproduced, or

(b) a recording of the whole or any part of a literary, dramatic or musical work from which sounds reproducing the work or part may be produced,

regardless of the medium on which the recording is made or the method by which the sounds are reproduced or produced. 

A sound is not protected as it is specifically sounds. Equally this creates something severable from a film in the case of a film with a sound track or indeed just the dialogue track severed from the film. 

VII. BROADCASTS 

Defined in the CDPA 1988 S.6(1) a broadcast is

an electronic transmission of visual images, sounds, or other information which - 

(a) is transmitted for simultaneous reception by mbmers of the public and is capable of being lawfully recieved by them, or

(b) is transmitted at a time determined solely by the person making the transmission for presentation to members of the public 

The transmission must be electronic, so it cannot be smoke signals. It is wired or wireless transmissions, and is broad in including visual images, sound and whantot. This section is largely just based on the statute. 

VIII. PUBLISHED EDITIONS

This is typographical arrangements of published editions. In Newspaper Licensing Agency v. marks & Spencer, L. HOffmann stated that edition is a product between covers that a publisher offers to the public. The typographical arrangement and appearances of the pages are protected. It also would protect new editions of public domain works.
