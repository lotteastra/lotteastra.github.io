title: Company Lecture Salomon v. Salomon
---

Case concerning a company which was liquidated, there was a loan, business went bad, defaulted on loan, and the loan was recovered by over 7k in unsecured credit went unpaid. A secured creditor was paid out from insolvency Salomon claimed beneficial ownership of the rest of the assets leaving the rest out in the cold. 

At first instance there was a judgement, the liquidator sought to argue fraud, that the initial sale by Salomon to the company, but that was unsuccessful. 

The judge at first instance found that the company was just an alias for Mr. Salomon and it was carrying on trade as an agent of Mr. Salomon. The principle must indemnify the debts of the agent. That was the principle. Salomon did not suceed at first instance.

The CA then heard the appeal. CA was more scathing and analyzed it based on trust principles. CA found that the way the company was used went against the principle of the statute, and Lindley LJ found that it was just a scheme to defraud creditors.

The act did not intend to sanctify this scheme. 

The statute was fully complied with. 

The HoL went off the statute alone in interpreting the law. They ruled that it was validly construed. They also found that a company is a separate legal person. Despite the motives or the owner of the shares. Not an agent, alias or trustee. 

They only have themselves to blame - Lord Magnahten Creditors

CA and HC were more moralistic in their judgement, whereas the HoL was more sympathetic. 

At that time they couldn't use Hansard in the courts. The Hansard did say though that the 7 member requirement was a backstop, and the CA was correct in terms of Parliament's intent, but HoL was literalistic. 

Salomon is a starting point in most cases regarding corporate personhood. 

'Foundational,' 

Implications can be unfair. 

Macura v. Northern Assurance co. -> Owned a timber estate, and then transferred it to the company which he also owned. By mistake he then took out an insurance policy on the tinder estate in his own name, there was a fire, and he sought a payout on the insurance. When he did the answer was no, since the timber was owned by the company and there could be no payout. 

Trade Credit Finance no. 1 ltd. v. Bligin -> Paragraph 68, the company's business is its own, even if the shareholders have total control over that business. 

Lee v. Lee's Air Farming ltd. -> Companies contract in their own name. Ran a crop dusting enterprise. Mr. Lee set up this company and he was the dominant shareholder, single director and the chief pilot. When his plane crashed he died. His widow sought compensation under New Zealand worker's compensation scheme. You can't be a worker and a director. The PC said that however he could be, just because he's a shareholder doesn't make him the employer, he's an employee as the chief pilot, since the company contracts its own workers. 

Hustwood Properties (A) ltd. & Ors v. Rossendale Borough Council & Anor -> A company has its own liabilities. If it has tax liabilities then the person owning the company does not inherit them.

This principle also applies for corporate groups/parent subsidiary relations

The Gramophone and Typewriter ltd. v. Stanley -> English company with a wholly owned subsidiuary in Germany. This German subsidiuary was very profitable. They wanted to tax the English company for the German profits. This could not be held to be the case and the tax was found not to be recoverable. 

Khan-Freund -> Describes Salomon as a 'calamitious decision,' companies were supposed to allow risky business and risky commercial ventures, however the company gets used by smaller businesses and as a result, it shields people from limited liability with no need to pool assets. A partnership would have been more appropriate. 

Detrimental to unsecured creditors. 

Erenst Lim -> Saloman is not really a landmark case because there was nothing really important about it. It was more that subsequent cases fed into it that it becmae important. 

Lipton -> Mythology of the case, allows corporate groups to juggle liability between each other, and shielding parent companies from its subsiduary. 

The notion is that Limited Liability allows evasion and wrongdoing to proliferate by shielding people. 

Second principle is limited liability, seperate from seperate legal personality.

Two principles 'Seperate Corporate Personhood,' and 'Limited Liability,' 

Argued that Limited Liability is the logical conclusion, but they did not arrive at the same time and there are companies without limited liability. 

Shareholders who are ordinary liable for debts left unpaid on their shares, then the liability is ordinarily limited. Shareholders are not liable for the debts of the company.

Consequences are that liabilites can only be met by the company's assets. You can't reach into the pockets of the shareholders. Ltd. or Plc. Is after the company's name to signal that a company is what is being dealt with. 

Big trading expeditions required limited liability. 

Bainbridge -> Limited liability and exmained it. Repeating what the judge said. More likely to undertake risks. Even if it wasn't in law, this would have been part of the bargain in commercial transactions, and negotiations would be done. Creditors also seek guarantees for their loans. 

Critics

Hansmann and Kraakman -> Unlimited liability for torts, limited liability should not exist for torts committed by the company. 

Muchinkski -> Allows shifting of costs onto creditors enterprise liability should exist within a corporate group to ensure that liability juggling cannot happen. 

Paddy Ireland -> Allows irresponsible actions by shareholders. 

Limited liability, voluntary creditors and involuntary creditors. 

Those who engage with the company should in theory be able to bargain for their rights. 

Smaller voluntary creditors do not have the bargaining power to get guarantees which leads to them being more unsecured creditors. 

Involuntary creditors, tort victims in particular, might be found out of luck for anything. A tort creditor cannot even in theory do anything. You cannot undertake a credit check. Tort law does give recourse but that is limited by the liability of the company.
