Piercing the corporate veil. 

Prest v. Petrodale -> It is when the courts violate the Saloman principle. 

This is when a person is said to be identified with the company by virtue of ownership and control. 

Until 1966 HoL could not overrule itself with the practice statement. 

Early cases do show that there is a willingness to look behind the corporate form. 

Dalmier co. ltd. v. Continental Tyre -> WW1 case where a German company had a British subsiduary. They said that a trade debt could not be enforced because trading with an enemy was not allowed. As far as the corporate form was concerned, the court was not impeded from investigating who the true actors were. 

Gilford Motor co. ltd. v. Horne -> Hold the controller liable for the company. Mr. Horne was employed by the company not allowed to solicit former customers after he left, he did this and set up his own company. The prior company tried to hold him to this and hold him liable. Tried to hide behind the Saloman principle, however the court decided that the company was just a front for him to breach his obligations. 

Jones v. Lipman -> In a contractual relationship over property. Before completion Mr. Lipman bought a company and transferred the property to the company to avoid having to hand it over, to defeat what would be a binding sale under contract law. The court did not buy this and saw through this. 

Littlewoods Mail Order Stores v. IRC -> Lord Denning is pro veil piercing, he found that courts could investigate the company and were not restrained by the Saloman principle. 

Wallersteiner v. Mair -> Lord Denning found this again here. 

Woolfson v. Strathclyde Regional Council -> Pushes back against Lord Denning, where the company is being used as a facade. 

Re a Company -> If it was just then the courts should be able to do so. Which is a very strange set of precedents. 

Acknowledged in the literature that there was no clear set of principles Lynn Gallagher and Peter Ziegler. 

Adams v. Cape Industries plc. -> CA in a long judgement sought to rationalize and make sense of this area. Cape industries parent company involved in the asbestos industry, mined asbestos by a bunch of south african companies, and a subsiduary that sells it in the UK, and another subsiduary that markets the goods in the US. Sale overseen where asbestos was sold to a factory in the US. As a result of the sale the workers at the factory sued Cape Industries as a group in Tort. First it was argued that under jurisdiction it cannot be sued. Eventually they settled out of court for $20 million. The aftermath was interesting. Cape Industries undertook strategic decisions to phase out its presence in the US fearing further litigation. 

Tyler 2 -> 200 claimants against Cape Industries, they didn't want to get involved and then there was default judgements against them. Keen to minimize presence in the US. Moving assets out of the US. Enforcement was a challenge in the UK. 

Success required Cape to be present in the US through its subsiduaries. It was tried to argue that the subsiduaries were a facade for cape, or that these businesses were doing business as an agent for Cape, and the claimants were unsuccessful, but the CA elaborated on the state of the law. 

CA held that there was no general principle to allow treating a company as a single economic unit, or contractually where they agree to be treated as a single economic unit, however outside of those derogations the court cannot pierce the Saloman principle. 

Next was facade, the court disagreed with the facade as there was legitimate dealings. The CA said that the facade point is where you could pierce the corporate veil but you cannot do so on the basis of justice. Only the facade. 

Agency, the theme was that there was no agency each subsiduary was operating as a business in its own right. 

This did not lead to consistency however.

The courts still took differing opinions. 

Trustor AB v. Smallbone & Ors no. 2 -> If a company was used as a device or facade to conceal the true facts thereby avoiding or concealing any liability of those individuals. 

Ben Hashem v. Al Shayf -> Frames six principles. Ownership and control is not sufficient in itself, nor is justice. There must be impropriety and that must be in relation to the company, where the owner uses it as a facade. It was also found that piercing the veil is to be invoked where necessary, a last resort remedy.

Prest v. Petrodel Resources -> The big one. Mr. Prest is an oil tycoon, and married to Mrs. Prest and the marriage broke down, she sought financial remedies in divorce and she wanted a number of properties. These properties were owned by companies which were then in turn owned by Mr. Prest, so she tried to pierce the veil in order to get the properties. She did not succeed on this argument. However SC held that the husband his arrangements were for his personal purposes however they were held under resultant trust and she was then entitled because of that. 

SC tried to argue what veil piercing is. It is the breakdown of corporate personality and disgregarding Saloman. Most authorities were examined and he felt that facade and sham was not helpful and that language was left behind, two principles were taken Evasion and Concealement. Evasion does not involve piercing the corporate veil, it was where the court wanted to see the true facts and looked behind to see the true facts but that was not disregarding separate legal personhood.

"Where there is an existing legal obligation or right, and a controller seeks to interpose a company in order to evade or frustrate the enforcement of that right, then the court can pierce the veil in order to deprive the controller of the advantage he would have had." That is the proper principle and the evasion principle to be used where necessary as a last resort. Lord Sumption. 

However the judges wrote more than one judgement. Lord Neuberger agreed with Lord Sumption, he was critical of veil piercing and questioned if it had actually been validly used. Argued in Horne and Jones, found that the same conclusion could have been reached without piercing. 

Lady Hale and Lord Wilson -> Company directors could not take unconscionable advantage of the people that they do business with. Examples of this idea in the case law. Tried to distinguish the circumstances where the company was liable for the director's act, or the director liable for the company's acts. 

Lord Mance said that the veil piercing should not be foreclosed to evasion and said that there may be other circumstances though those would be extremely rare.

Lord Clarke agreed with Sumption but agreed on future circumstances with Lord Mance.

Lord Walker said that Piercing is not a doctrine. It is just a label used where a company is identified with its controllers. 

Aftermath of Prest 

Courts struggled greatly to distinguish between evasion and concealement principle. Concealement is just looking behind the company to see the state of affairs rather than disregarding corporate personhood. Legally banal to apply ordinary legal principles. 

The older terminology is still used even after Prest. 

Hurstwood Properties A ltd. & Ors v. Rossendale Borough Council & Anor -> In the UK commercial properties are subject to a tax called non-domestic rates. There have been attempts to avoid having to pay them, and these rates have been crippling hohwever there was a scheme in this case to dodge non-domestic rates. A shell company would have the property transferred to it. It would incur those rates, but before the bill could be paid then the company would be liquidated and the liabilities would dissipate. The real owners would not pay anything. Local authorities hated this a lot. They sought action in Hurstood Properties. 

Ramsay Principle and Veil Piercing. Local authorities tried to pierce so that the true owners would be liable. Tax point did succeed but the veil piercing did not.

SC found something interesting and investigated veil piercing in more details. They found two parts, forward piercing where the claimants seek to have the company liable for the controllers for the debts actions and obligations of the company.

Reverse veil piercing is where you go after the controllers and then try to find the company accountable for its controllers. 

SC in the real cases they were all reverse piercing cases. Where the company where the company was liable for the actions of the controllers. 

Reverse veil piercing was found to be more existing. 

They also found that it was not a seperate doctrine following Lord Walker's point. 

The evasion principle did not technically apply in Hurstwood. Trying to avoid a future liability not a past one.

Creasey v. Breachwood -> Employee wrongfully dismissed from a welding company, knowing this, the two directors tried to reorganize their affairs, they transferred the assets to another company and then wound up the company that Mr. Creasey was fired from wrongfully. He wanted to substitute one company for another, trying to get the court to see through this corporate reorganization, and in this case the court did allow this and find for Mr. Creasy. 

Ord v. Belhaven Pubs ltd. -> Couple that took a 20 year lease of a pub. Things did not go as advertised. They purchased it on the belief that it was a profitable business, and they found they were induced by misrepresentation to buy this lease. Shortly after this case was brought the group reogranized and moved assets aroundn as a result of financial pressures. So in the legal proceedings they tried to substitute one company for another. And they could not in this case and Creasey was overturned, and there was a reaffirmation of Saloman. No sham or facade in this analysis. Treat every company as a seperate legal person was the primary ratio. However the courts understood the public policy implications of this could be problematic. 

There was a time to circumvent this principle. 

Chandler v. Cape plc. -> Employee who worked for a subsiduary of Cape, concerning an asbestos business. Claimant suffered asbestos injuries as a result of personal injury. The judge said that this was possible in Tort. Rejected that this is about veil piercing, this is about a duty of care and an assumption of responsibility which gives rise to a special relationship between the employee and the parent and the subsiduary. 

1. First does the parent and subsiduary are in a relevent respect the same for the business. 

2. The parent has or ought to have superior knowledge on some relevant aspect of health and safety in the particular industry. 

3. The subsiduary's system of work is unsafe as parent company knew or ought to have known and

4. Parent know or ought to have foreseen that the subsiduary or its employees would rely on its using that superior knoweldge for the employee's protection.

This did not open up the floodgates. 

Thompson v. Renwick Group -> Reached the opposite conclusion in another asbestos case. One of the directors of the parents sat on the board of the subsiduary, and that was not sufficient to say that the parent had control over the health and safety of the subsiduary. The examination is fact dependent.

This did not stop with employees.

Lord Briggs -> Parent can owe a duty, Paragraph 49, depends on the facts and the degree of control by the parent against the subsiduary. Can these give rise to a duty of care. Effective group wide policies can give a duty of care. Paragraph 49-56 Vedanta. Key factors to establish duty of care. 

Okpabi v. Royal Dutch Shell plc. -> Oil spills caused by the subsiduary. Lord Hamblen reaffirmed Vedanta. You shouldn't be using Caparo but the factors in Vedanta. CA said that group wide policies cannot give rise to a duty of care, but in Vedanta it was.
