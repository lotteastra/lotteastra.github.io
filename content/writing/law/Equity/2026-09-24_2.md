1. How does the general approach to the application of the law differ in Equity from that of the Common Law

Equity is described as more flexible than common law. In that it grew out of the rigidity of Common Law courts. In this respect however, it is flexible in that it lends rigidity to promises which could have otherwise been ignored but where ignoring those promises would be 'unconscionable.' This notion of conscionability is central to Equity being its genesis and the justification for the various different remedies that it can afford. 

2. What did John Seldon say was "as long as the Chancellor's foot"? What did he mean by this?

Because Equity was justified by reference to conscience and indeed religiously being administered by the Lord Chancellor, an originally ecclisiastical role, what was conscionable to one chancellor may be different to another. As such the size of the conscience may vary as much as the variation in shoe size between any two given individuals. 

3. How equitable is Equity? What does it have to do with fairness? 

It is about giving effect to promises that may be malformed in a legal sense but where it would be unconscionable, or unfair, to not carry them through. Though as Equity has developed over time into a clear doctrine it has cast off parts of this concern for fairness. See Farepak. It is more concerned with that there is a clear promise and the clear operation of a clear promise in line with the three certainties. 

4. What are trusts and why do people create them?

A trust is where property is legally held by another, but where the benefits of that property are intended for someone other than the legal holder. You might create a trust for various reasons, where the intended beneficiary is unable to hold it, as was the case under coveture for daughters in wealthy families getting married, or seeking to hold realestate while being under 18. There may also be pragmatic reasons, for example in an investment fund where the fund manager is better placed to manage the property in order to attain greater benefit for the beneficiary than if they held it otherwise, equally if the beneficiary is too young to adequately manage the sums that are intended to benefit them. The reasoning for a trust is dependent on context, see the Lady Hale quote. 

5. Who are the three main actors in an express trust relationship?

Settlor/Testator who set aside the assets to create the trust, the trustee who legally holds the property but does so on behalf of, the beneficiary who benefits from the property.

6. What is the difference between a testamentary trust and an inter vivos trust?

Inter vivos, between the living, the settlor is still alive. Testamentary with the dead, where the testator is dead. 

7. According to F.W. Maitland, the trust is Equity's greatest invention. Why is this?

In Maitland's words 'It is an ' institute ' of great elasticity and generality ; as
elastic, as general as contract.' The first use of trusts was for Monks who could not hold any property but were able to do so through the mechanism of a trust. A mechanism which did not develop in other jurisdictions. 

A trust creates flexibility that allows different structures of ownership for a variety of purposes and allows those flexible agreements between people to be enforceable, and not merely be based on trust which in turn makes them far more attractive and indeed feesible to use between people who may not necessarily trust each other. In a sense a trust exists so as to make trust a less necessary component within a private bargain.
