Questions
1. How do the courts decide whether a settlor/testator intends to create a trust?

They examine the specific words used by the settlor for inter vivos trusts and the testator/testatrix for testamentory trusts.

Every trust following from the case of Knight v. Knight must conform to the three certainties. Arguably also a fourth certainty following Boyce v. Boyce. It should be noted that the minimum level of certainty required is necessary. For creating an express trust there must be certainty of intention, and at this point the specific words used are of great import to distinguish a trust from a gift, typically this means looking at the specific words used and if those words could be used to construe an obligation.

For example in Re Adams v. Kennsington Vestry it was held that words to the effect of 'do what is right with regards to the children,' created a moral obligation and those words were not strong enough to create a trust, whereas, in Paul v. Constance words that were 'as much yours as mine,' were sufficient to create a trust as it created that obligation. Comiskey v. Bowing Hadry also constituted an obligation as it was a long preamble that went beyond merely hoping or that moral language and gave what is in effect quite a specific obligation. The specific words used may also not matter and rather the intention behind them, in Re Steels Trusts, the wording hugged the wording of a prior case Shelly v. Shelly and while the court commented that the words in Shelly would not have been certain enough to create a trust today, because there was a trust found in that case, then it would follow that a trust was intended to follow that prior case. Unusual but demonstrates what the courts think of when finding certainty of intention. 

The intention must also not be a sham intention, in Midland PLC bank v. Wyatt, it was found that there was a trust with a certain intention but that trust was certainly created in order to try and outwit creditors and protect assets. Similarly in Webb v. Webb while the trust was validly created in that it fulfilled the three certainties, the intention was a sham as the effect of that instrument was to hide assets while enabling the settlor to retain strong discretionary rights, which showed the true intention of the trust, and therefore undid the whole instrument. 

Equally the subject matter must be certain.

2. What is the relationship between certainty of intention and certainty of subject matter?

Both need to exist for a trust to be validly created. There must be a certainty that this specific subject matter will be brought into a trust. 

3. Was Dillon LJ's judgment in Hunter v Moss well-reasoned? Can it be reconciled with Re Goldcorp?

It was not well reasoned. The reasoning was better explained in Harvard where shares are not chattels, and the particular quality of a share needs to be examined and distinguished from a chattel. A share is fungible, and the value of each share is the same to the point where no two shares can be distinguished from one another. This uniformity means that certainty of subject matter can be made out without the need for segregation. 

4. Bob, a novelist, died recently. Consider the validity of each of the following dispositions in his will and advise the trustees of the will, Tim and Gale, accordingly: 

(a) ‘£100,000 to my brother Louis, in absolute confidence that he will use the money to look after our mother in her time of need’;

This wording is analogous to the wording of Re Adams v. Kensington Vestry, but also similar to Comiskey v. Bowing Hadry, 'will make use of the money,' in particular confers a strong obligation, and equally what is clear is that this is a discretionary trust, as it is for Louis to judge when the mother is in a 'time of need,' so there is an obligation on the money contingent on that 'will,' which shifts it from a moral to a legal obligation. We can also distinguish from Re Adams where it was said that the property was conveyed for 'the absolute use,' instead of 'absolute confidence.' Therefore the intention to create a trust is likely clear and clearly distributed. Furthermore the subject matter is clear, and the specific intention of what is to be done with the money creates a discretionary trust. 

(b) ‘I direct my trustees to hold the bulk of my residuary estate on trust for my wife, Nora; 

Trust is clearly construed here so intention is not the issue, see Knight v. Knight, and Paul v. Constance. The issue here is 'bulk of residuary estate.' Palmer v. Simmonds is the principle authority for this area, where 'bulk,' was not sufficiently certain enough. The specific subject matter of the trust was not segregated and could not be established, it is not clear enough. Therefore this trust would fail. 

(c) ‘Three of my five Rembrandt sketches to be kept safe for my niece Saskia, when she reaches the age of 18’; 

This is a dangerous place to be. In David Wilde's article, he talks of 'distributional certainty,' and this is the crux of the issue here. In his article he mentions the case of Boyce v. Boyce where there were two houses to be held on trust, with one of the nieces to pick one house, and the other being given a trust over the other, however when the first niece died,this was not sufficiently certain as to be able to be constituted as a trust since it was unclear which house would be held on trust since the machinery of distribution had failed. In this case it is unclear which 3 of the five, and even where the subject matter is fungible, in the case of Goldcorp or the wine case, because the assets were not already segregated then that would be unable to constitute a trust. 

While there is an indication of future segregation because the subject matter has not been segregated prior to the creation of the trust, then it cannot operate. An argument could be made that the since this is on death then the future segregation of the sketches could be sufficient, but that still begs the question of distributional certainty, which subjects to who, and therefore this future segregation would not be able to defeat this impediment. Though in Hunter v. Moss fungible intangible property was held to be able to constitute a trust even when not segregated, but that is because all the assets are the same, and this was elaborated in Harvard Securities, if they were all the same Rembrandt sketch made on the same day with the same value then perhaps applying Hunter then that could be sufficiently certain to constitute a trust, but this would really stretch the court and Hunter has not been free from criticism, as such it is unlikely a trust could be constituted in this case. 

(d) ‘The rights to half the novels I have written to be held on trust for Miriam’. 

Certainty of intention is here, see Knight v. Knight. Not at issue. There is also no indication of sham intention as this is a testamentory trust and there do not appear to be any creditors at issue. 

This relates to intangible property so there is more scope for Hunter to be applied, and in Boyce v. Boyce, the issue was that the specific machinery of creating the trust was unable to operate but it could operate in this case. Half is after all an exact amount, but half of what rights exactly. A group of published novels will not all have the same value so perhaps it could be a trust based on each novel, split so that half of the value was put on trust for M. This would however be the court interveneing to add its own words here. As such there's no indication of which half has been segregated so Goldcorp could apply here and it would fail for lack of certainty of subject matter. However, it would depend on how divisible these specific rights were, if they could be divided such that half the value, which is again a substitution the court may be unwilling to make, that could create a trust. Even so it would be a strecth and as a result, it is unlikely that a trust would be constituted from this, but not impossible depending on the breadth of Hunter v. Moss. Again the problem of distributional certainty is at issue here. 

For gifts the term is donor, donee.

To constitute a trust there needs to be directions for the trustees in order to confer a legal obligation. Look at all the words used and try to truely construe them. 

Gold v. Hill -> Look after is not sufficiently certain. 

Tito v. Warddell -> The word trust is not necessary nor even sufficient in order to constitute a real trust.

Obligations must be for specific property and not vague majoritarian ideas. 

For intangibles then it is a two step analysis

1. Is it intangible?

2. Are all of the parts of it homogenous and fungible

If Yes to both then Hunter v. Moss

If No to any then Goldcorp
