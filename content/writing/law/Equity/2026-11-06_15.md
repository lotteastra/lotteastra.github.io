The three certainties are required. Trusts can be orally declared, implied, express intention, wills, in writing. 

It is important because of how trusts operate so that the trust can run in the first place because of the significant obligations implied. 

Gifts also have certainties.

A general intention to benefit is enough to construe a gift. The courts will try to give effect to express intention.

Pearson v. Lehman Brothers -> Law does not lightly allow parties; purposes and intentions to be defeated by supposed uncertainty.

A necessary consequence of intnetion that the parties should share property beneficially in circumstances where the parties themselves have given no thought at all to the terms of the consequential trust. If indeed they even recognized its existence.

!Certainty of Intention!

The settlors intention. Express intention to create a trust whether in lifetime or after the settlor's death. 

That does not mean the settlor must use the words trust or specifically that they are intending to create a trust. 

Settlor must have capacity to create a trust.

If settlor wishes to give a gift then they must have the intention to give a gift.

Look at the wording. 

The key test is can we conclude that the intention was to impose mandatory obligation.

Tito v. Waddell no. 2 -> the word of trust is not necessary to create a trust, but the specific wording is vital.

Power is not mandatory. Mere powers and fiduciary powers. Certain tests are applicable to both. 

Paul v. Constance -> 'as much yours as mine,' seperate bank account created a trust obligation. 

Jones v. Lock -> I give this cheque to baby, i am going to put it away for him. Not sufficient to create a trust but a failed gift.

R v. Cloves no. 2 -> Typically creditor debtor.
