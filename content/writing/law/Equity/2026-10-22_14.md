1.     To what extent does the definition of charitable purposes align with ordinary perceptions of the notion of charitable activity? 

It does a bit. Charity in general life means generosity, seeking to help your fellow person, and the first cases on this point towards the same impulse and. The question is how we give formalities to that same impulse. 

2.     How has the decision in Independent Schools Council v The Charity Commission influenced the meaning of the ‘public benefit’ requirement?

It has to benefit some portion of the general public directly. It was insufficient to say that just relieving the school system was sufficient. 

3.     In what circumstances can a charitable organisation engage in political activity? 

It has to be incidental and not the primary purpose of the charity. 

R v. Radio Authority ex Parte Bull -> Many charitable trusts today national charities concerned with children or the physically or mentally disabled or with housing the homeless for example are prominent in their espousal of political means to attend their ends.

'political campaigning or political activity must be undertaken by a charity only in the context of the furtherance of its charitable aims.' Charity commission

Bowman v. Secular Society -> Lord Parker "A trust for the attainment of political objects has always been held invalid not becuase it is illegal for everyone is at liberty ot advocate or promote any lawful means a change in the law but because the court has no means of judging whether a proposed change in the law will or will not be for a public benefit." Parker at 442

5.    The Sanatorium in Stoke Newington is fully equipped with the latest medical and surgical equipment and is staffed by doctors and nurses whose salaries are approximately five times higher than those earned by public sector healthcare professionals. Patients pay an exorbitant daily rate to stay at the Sanatorium, in addition to the fee agreed for their specific procedure or treatment. However, it maintains very high occupancy rates (there is rarely more than one empty room at any one time). 

The Sanatorium is governed by a board of governors each of whom is paid an annual fee for their services. The Chief Executive earns £500,000 per year and is supported by a large administrative team. Any profits made by the Sanatorium are reinvested in the business and, according to its mission statement, all employees are committed to improving the health and wellbeing of the Sanatorium’s patients. All employees are encouraged to join the Sanatorium’s generous private pension scheme and, if they have school age children, they are entitled to send their offspring to the Sanatorium’s own independent school which is run from a neighbouring building. 

Virtually all the Sanatorium’s patients are ultra-high net worth individuals who come from abroad to access its world-leading services in several areas of medicine. Sometimes, the Sanatorium makes significant financial contributions to sympathetic political parties and, occasionally, instructs lobbying firms to make sure that the UK government remains receptive to the interests of Britain’s world-class healthcare providers and the medical needs of foreign elites. 

The Sanatorium’s board of governors has just passed a motion enabling it to seek charitable status. Advise the board as to whether such an application would be successful and recommend any steps that the Sanatorium might take to help in this regard. 




The Operative Case here is Council of Independent Schools which gives some of the ways around this.

So for a charity there are two primary aspects it must be EXCLUSIVELY CHARITABLE and FOR PUBLIC BENEFIT

The charitable purpose in this case which they would seek would be promotion of health. This is a recognized charitable purpose under S.3(1)(d) in the charities act 2011. However the large board and huge pay indicates that the purpose is to make money for its employees and the charitable aims to promote health are incidental. The charitable aim must be the exclusive aim and making a successful business is not the same thing. It would be necessary to argue that these private benefits are in pursuance of the charity's aim, so perhaps a reference to the need for specific talent guaranteed by such a salary etc. could allow it to have a charitable purpose.

Nuffield Health v. London Borough of Merton -> Gyms advanced health but members paid 80ppm with no reductions for the poor and limited non-member services. Below public benefit threshold. 

Independent Schools Council v. Charity COmmission -> Where the benefit is to a section of the public the opportunity to benefit must be unreasonably restricted by the ability to pay any fees charged. 

The purpose must be beneificial to the public (not detrimental or at least more beneficial than detrimental)

The benefit must be avaiable to the public at large or to a sufficient section of the community

Public Benfit -> The authorities outline that the benefit must not be foreclosed from the general public and as such access must be granted to more than an incidental number of the general public. So fees waived for certain people and to allow those in poverty to benefit as well.
