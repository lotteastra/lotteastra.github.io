The three certainties

1. Certainty of Intention

2. Certainty of Subject Matter

3. Certainty of Object

CERTAINTY OF INTENTION

Trusts can be created in writing through writing, an express trust. Orally declared as well. Implied trusts are created by the court. Express vs. Implied. 

The three certainties are crucial for finding ANY trust, express or implied. 

Knight v. Knight -> Establishes the three certainties for a trust to be valid.

These requirements are also important for gifts.

These certainties are important to establish what a trust. It needs to be possible to ascertain that these three certainties were crucial. The court cannot act without these.

A trust creates significant legal obligations so when the trust was created is also crucial. 

Third parties may also be impcated by a trust. For example in insolvency and also in tax implications. 

The test for certainty of intention is that the intention is imperative.

The test for the subject matter is certainty.

The test for the object is also certainty. 

A trust imposes obligations. Even in a discretionary trust, it imposes an obligation. If there is discretion on if the asset can be distributed or not then this is not a trust. Looking for EXPRESS intention. Courts will make an effort to meet those express intentions, but if the wording does not allow then that will fail. 

Pearson v. Lehman Bros. -> Look at if there is intention to create an obligation. 

Inter vivos trust and testamentory trust. The word trust need not be used. We look for the intention and if there was an intention to create an obligation to use the assets for someone else. 

The settlor needs to be capable of creating a trust, so not a minor. 

In contrast with gifts, there needs to be a general intention to benefit the recipient but that is not good enough to create a trust. This is a question of facts. 

The key test is if the creator wanted to impose a mandatory obligation on someone to hold property for the benefit of another so that they are under a duty to do so. 

Tito v. Waddell no. 2 -> The word trust is not necessary for the creation of a trust, merely the intention to create an obligation of this nature is sufficient. 

The court however interprets from behaviour and documentation. 

Paul v. Costance -> Separated from wife, and then started a relationship with the claimant he created a new bank account and said that the money in that account is for both of them, 'as much yours as mine.' He deposits their joint bingo winnings into that account. The wife says that it was her account since there was no will, but the court held that the money was on trust for the lover. 

How do we work out intention? It's an objective assessment. What a reasonable person would conclude. 

Precatory words might create a problem where it is just a desire or a wish but not enough to create an obligation. Hope wish desire are insufficient. Lack of ceretainty of intention.

Re Adams Cotton LJ at 410 'I think that some of the older authorities went a deal too far in holding that some particular words appearing in a will were sufficient to create a trust. Undoubtedly, confidence if the rest of the context shows that a trust is intented may make a trust, but what we have to look at is the whole of the will which we have to construe, and if the confidence is that she will do what is right as regards the disposal of the property, I cannot say what that is, on the true construction of the will, a trust imposed upon her.' 

Comiskey v. Bowring-Habury -> Emphasis on construction of the document as a whole. 'Absolutely in full confidence that she [the widow] will make such use of the property as I would have made myself and that at her death she will devise it to such one or more of my nieces as she may think fit.' 

The words were enough to constitute a strong obligation through their being specific.

Abuse of trust

There may be times where a trust is validly created but ineffective because the trust is considered to be a sham or is illusory or even illegal. 

Sham trusts: Where the settlor may have intended to create a trust but there is an ulterior motive to mislead creditors, tax authorities or hide assets in divorce. 

Illusory Trusts: The settlor believes to be creating a trust but the nature of the obligations are outside of a trust. 

If there is no certainty of intention then there can be no trust. However what happens to the asset depends on the reasons for failure of intention. It could return to the settlor or it may be treated as an absolute gift. If everything and truely everything else fails then it will go to the crown.

CERTAINTY OF SUBJECT MATTER

Which asset is the trust concerned with? Trusts are not companies so they cannot hold things by themselves. We need to say which asset specifically it is a question of fact. Can it be ring fenced off from all other assets or can the asset be worked out. 

A trust can be declared over many different kind of assets

Palmer v. Simmonds -> Leave the 'bulk,' of my residuary estate unto a certain named person. Was this certain enough. No it wasn't certain enough.

Re Last -> 'Anything that is left,' is sufficiently certain because it is following a list of other steps and therefore it can be worked out objectively.

Re Golay's wills trust -> 'Reasonable income,' is sufficiently certain as it can be determined objectively court can use it on the basis of the named person's previous standard of living. Determinable through objective facts. 

Kindersley VC -> "When a person is said to have given the bulk of his property, what is meant is not the whole but the greater part. [...] when therefore the testatrix uses that term, can I say she has used a term expressing a definite clear certain part of her estate, or the whole of her estate? I am bound to say she has not designated the subject as to which she expresses her confidence and I am therefore of the opinion that there is no trust created." 

Be careful of the descriptive words used for an asset. My favourite house, my best car, my beloved painting, might not be clear enough. It might be difficult to determine what is being referred to. It will fail without other evidence. 

The trust property must exist at the time the trust was established it cannot be over future property.
