Constitution of gifts and trusts

1. Rules that relate to proper constitution of express trusts

2. Situations were those rules have not been satisfied and should equity intervene

3. Rules on constitution are overlapping with those that relate to gifts

If a donor has not complied with the steps for making a gift, should equity intervene to save a gift that is defective and if it does under what circumstances and why.

A trust will be executed when it is expressed. The trust property has vested in the intended trustees or trustee. Involves a process where the trust property is transferred to third party trustees and vests in them. Proper constitution is the point of no return

Methods of various types Method 1

Settlor transfers the property to the trustee, and if they have not done so already then they are obligated to. The beneficiary can enforce against the trustees or the settlor. 

Until the moment of constitution it is just a gratuity and equity will not assist them. Because they are a volunteer. 

Constitution moment of no return. Inter vivos gifts and trusts there can be problems because when constitution happens. 

Formalities and constitution are different.

In order to transfer property there are formalities that must be satisfied. If the transfer relates to a chattel then there are no formalities. Some types of property there might be formalities. There is an overlap but they're not the same things.

Classic situation for gifts and trusts. There are three recognized modes.

Milroy v. Lord -> CA 

Donor can make an absolute gift by transferringn the legal title to the donee. (Absolute gift)

Settlor transfers the legal title of the property to a third party trustee for the purpsoe of holding on trust for another. (Express trust method 1)

Settlor declares that he or she holds property on trust for another (Express trust method 2) self declare themselves to be a trustee for another. 

Milroy v. Lord -> In order to create a settlement binding on the settlor they must do everything that must be done according to the nature of the property in order for the settlement to bind them. 

What is necessary depends on the nature of the property. 

There may be requirements whenever property is transferred. When a settlor self declares there's no need to transfer as part of the constitution and in a way that is simpler but it can raise issues. 

As far as the settlor is concerned if they're the absolute owner, that declaration of trust is an event. Before that event did they have any equitable rights to hold for another. If you are an absolute owner you have non equitable rights. You cannot be your own trustee when you are legally entitled. 

Westdeusche v. Islington BC -> A person solely entitled to the full beneficial ownershiip of money or property both at law and in equity does not have equitable interest in that property. The legal title carries with it all rights unless and until there is a separation of the legal and equitable estates. There is no separate equitable title. 

Method 2, no prior equitable rights. The event of the trust creates a separation. 

There is a twist for Method 2 There was a situation in 

Choithram International v. Pagarani -> Odd set of facts where the settlor had wanted to establish an unincorporated association in the British virgin islands made of various shares. He wanted to establish this foundation on his deathbed. He wanted to transfer the shares for them. The formalities had not been satisfied and he directed the trustees orally to hold them for the benefit of the foundation. If they were third parties then it would not have been properly constituted. However there were numerous trustees one of which who was the settlor. 

Privy Council recognized that Pagarani did not fit in the established categories, but they said it would be unconscionable to deny the gift. It did not have separate legal personality. It will not strive officiously to deny a gift. It was held that there was no difference between self declaration where there was a number of trustees including the settlor, and it was just the settlor, and there was no need to comply with the formalities. Not a prerequisite for an enforceable trust. 

There may be requirements in order to vest property within a third party which there would not be for a settlor who acts as trustee.

Milroy v. Lord -> Declared his intention to create a trust over shares in a bank, executed a deed, so that the other person would hold those shares on trust for the benefit for another. The formalities were stock transfer forms to affect the disposition. Those stock transfer forms needed to go to the company to then be registered. Those things had to happen. If those things hadn't been done then it had not been properly constituted. The settlor had not completed them, so there was no transfer in an orthodox sense. However the perspective beneficiary Milroy tried to enforce it. The settlor gave the trustee power of attorney to affect the steps to be taken, but the trustee had not done so. Question for the court was should equity intervene. The beneficiary was a volunteer. The court found there was clear intention but not everything had been done in accordance with the nature of the property in question to create a valid trust. Equity would not intervene because even though they could have done those steps, because they had not been done equity would not intervene. 

Beyond those three situations there is no equity to perfect an imperfect gift.

Equity will however intervene in some situations and some things have moved on. 

Richards v. Delbridge -> Inter vivos gift of leasehold business premesis to grandson. This deed and all thereto belonging I tive to my grandson from this time forth with all stock in trade. Failed gift for non-observance of formalities. Can equity intervene to save a defective gift. Grandfather held it as a leasehold but to transfer a lease one would need to comply with what is now the S.25 of the LPA 1925, and there was a forunner provision. He scribbled words on the back of the deed. The valid deed for himself. He handed that to his daughter, the grandson was a minor at the time. No mention of the leasehold in the will. The grandson challenged the defective gift. Could equity intervene? One of the arguments submitted was that it failed as a gift but maybe it could be a trust. Did the grandfather declare himself as a trustee over the leasehold property for the benefit of the grandson. 

Lord Jessel MR found it to be not a trust because there was failure to show intention to that end. Equity will not perfect an imperfect gift either. The classic position there. 

Westdeusche v. Islington BC -> In the case of a trust the conscience of the owner requires him to carry out the purposes for which the property was vested in him or her or which the law imposes on him by reason of his or her unconscionable conduct or outcomes. Lord Browne-Wilkinson. Where the outcome is unconscionable then equity may intervene. This is a constructive trust. 

Re Rose -> More relaxed attitude in the 20th century. CA decision in 1952. We are concerned with the 1952 decision. The facts, a donor who wanted to make a gift of shares to his wife and he wanted to do this before death. He completed the forms relating to the shares. In 30th March 1943 he completed them, and he submitted them to the company for the purposes of registering the transfer. Company recieved them but did not register them right away and they stayed for 3 months, registered on the 30th of June 1943, but unfortunately Mr. Rose died in 1947, but when the date was that when submitted no IHT, but if it was when it was registered then it would attract IHT. So it mattered when the registration occurred. CA when was the effective moment of transfer. They looked at the facts and the law. The classic situation Milroy v. Lord, had not happened until the transfer had occured not binding. However Re Rose took more lax view. CA said that the settlor had done everything he could do, the last act was contingent on the company officers, and the settlor was reliant on a third party to complete the transaction. Mr. Rose had done everything in his power and that was sufficient. 

CA reached a decision and saved the imperfect gift, the moment of transfer was held to be March not June. Tax was not owed. He was trying to make a gift and not become a trustee. How does equity perfect the imperfect gift? Equity recognized that there was a gap. In that three month gap who owned the shares? Mr. Rose was not an express trustee during that period, however it treats Mr. Rose as remaining the legal owner as a trustee for the wife being a beneficiary, and this is a constructive trust with no formalities. In this period there is a mechanism where equity can intervene in this period. It would be unconsciounable to deny the gift. So a constructive bare trust is a device to allow courts to intervene. 

Mascall v. Mascall -> Transfer of land with registered title where donor sought to resile from gift. A father planned to give house to son as a gift, and completed the certificate for land transfer, but he wanted to withdraw from the gift after completing the form. The son had the relevant documentation, the land transfer form, but the son had not given the paperwork to the land registry. The gift would be upheld because the father had done everything he could to affect the transfer. He could have chosen to have done everything to affect all of the steps, however the court appreciated that the father had done all he needed to do having regard to the nature of the property and it was for the donee to submit it. The court endorsed Re Rose and its approach. In Re Rose Mr. Rose could not do what was needed, but the father could have done, so it is a minimal threshold. The normal situation was that the donee submits to the land registry.

Grondona v. Steffel -> Decision land transfer was upheld because the failure of the transfer was due to negligence of the solicitors in that case. 

Pennington v. Waine -> Unconscionability. Builds on the shift in case law. Upheld defective gift in shares, despite that the donor had not done everything, the court held that what was important if it would be unconscionable for the donor to withdraw. In this case the donor was an aunt of the recipient donee. Donor owned a lot of shares 1,500 out of 2,000, intending to give 400 shares to her nephew, and then the nephew would become director and it was in line with that decision. She filled out the forms, and then submitted them to the auditor. Not to the company. The auditor placed them in the files and took the steps to ensure Harold was appointed director. The aunt then made a will and there was no mention of the 400 shares. She believed that she had already transferred them. The problem was that the 400 shares remained in the company's files and had not been registered. So the inter vivos transfer had not happened. So unless they were transferred they would be the residuary estate. Unlike Re Rose where they had done everything in their power, the aunt had not submitted them to someone who was able to register them. The auditor was the aunt's agent. This factual situation was more like Milroy than Re Rose. Should equity perfect the gift? She hadn't done everything in her power. CA was sympathetic to the situation as it existed based on the facts. CA held in the end that equity would intervene to save the shares. The forms were properly completed. Both parties believed that they had done all that they needed to do. The auditor had assured them there was nothing more. Also for him to be director he would have needed at least one share without the gift he would not have been director. CA found that the imperfect gift would be upheld. Equitable interest in the shares from the moment of transfer. Constructive bare trust. CA engaged with the issue of unconscionability developing from Pagarani, equity will not strive to defeat a gift. Fourfold justification, that the aunt had intended to make an immediate gift, she had told him of this intention, she had given them to the auditor who advised them that there were no necessary additional steps, the donee had taken up the role of director on reliance of these shares. Despite the circumstances equity will intervene. Unconscionable if no intervention. Not easy to see detrimental reliance. 

How do we know what would be conscionable or unconscionable? Reliance on the facts? Detrimental reliance? High watermark decision. 

Stepping away from unconscionability from Pennington

Zettal v. Kaye -> CA decision in 2011 Zettal died owning shares, express trust over the only two shares of a company. Third party nominees were the trustees, he transferred the shares and intended to give them to the partner, and completed one form, and handed a blank transfer form for the second. CA decided that the first share was filled in, that was sufficient for a constructive trust for the partner but not the second, the blank form was not enough. Not done everything in the power. CA it could not be said that it was not unconscionable to deny the gift. 

Curtis v. Pullbrook -> Family dispute arising from disputed shares. Equity would not intervene to perfect the imperfect gift. Going back from Pennington. Three situations where equity would intervene. First is Re Rose exception where the donee has done everything in their power. Second is where the donee has suffered detrimental reliance. Equity is bound to uphold the gift because it would be unconscionable to deny it. Third where the gift can be upheld through benevolent construction or an express trust. Order from Pennington. 

Latest case is 

Khan v. Mahmood -> Nephew wanted to make a gratuitous transfer of interest in a co-owned house to uncle. Non observance of formalities required for transfer. Unconscionable to allow resile from the gift. Focussed on Donee's conduct rather than Donee's reliance on donors intentions, intentions are revealed through his actions. 

Commentary that unconscionability is bound up in estoppel and not a free-standing doctrine and therefore no reliance means no unconscionability. Not an unconscionable situation. Unilateral unconscionability is used in the judgement. 

Incompletely constitutued trusts

Sometimes a beneficiary is not a volunteer. Or when a beneficiary is party to a deed which provides consideration.

Canon v. Hartley -> Settled with wife and daughter to pass on any sums he inherits onto his daughter. Failed to pass it on in accordance with the deed. Therefore fine.

Contract Rights of THird Parties Act -> Another thing that can be invoked

Rule in Strong v. Bird -> Extinction of debt between the parties to extend to gifts as well as debts. 'IF it can be shown that the donor intended to make an immediate gift to an inter vivos donee, and that intention endures until death, if the donee is appointed to the role of executor then that combination will mean that the gift will be perfected. Takes effect on death of the donor.'
