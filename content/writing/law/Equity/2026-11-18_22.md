 1. “A declaration of trust in respect of land which is not evidenced in writing is valid but unenforceable”. What is meant by such a statement and how does it impact on the functioning of section 53(1)(b) LPA?  

!ANSWER
 The trust is valid but not enforceable without court intervention. In this sense the court can go back and treat as binding a trust that was not created validly so as to protect against fraud. See Rochefold v. Boustead.

2. Read Douglas’ article (‘Trusts, Formalities and the Doctrine in Rochefoucauld v Boustead’). Why does the author argue that an express trust arose on the facts of this case rather than a constructive trust? Does it really matter, in your opinion?  

!ANSWER
A constructive trust does not arise because it would be difficult to put the timing of it appropriately, since constructive trusts arise 'when the last fact necessary for its creation,' arises and when that fact might be may be a frought preposition. In Bannister v. Bannister a constructive trust was found in that case and that was another way of sidestepping the evidential requirement, but in that case it was an eviction and the Claimant's position had not been altered by the time the last fact necessary arose. However in Rochefoucauld v. Boustead the Claimant's position had deteriorated by the time of the unconscionable conduct so it would be hard to really determine things. 

3. What does the decision in Vandervell (No. 1) tell us about the operation of section 53(1)(c) LPA 1925?

!ANSWER
S.53(1)(c) is about creating traceability of beneficial interests and so long as those interests can be traced by a disposition of a legal title then they need not be attested to in writing. 

4. Rachel and her brother, Brian, were the beneficiaries of a family trust. The trust assets consisted of: (a) the family home known as ‘Bleak House’ (Rachel and Brian being beneficial joint tenants of the property); (b) 1,000 shares in Pear Ltd.  

Brian wanted to purchase Rachel’s interest in the trust. She orally agreed to sell her shares in Pear Ltd to him for £1 million. However, Rachel told Brian that she intended to keep her interest in Bleak House for the benefit of Karen, the daughter of her best friend, Sarah (Karen is 16 years old). Brian agreed and paid Rachel the sum of £1 million, although no documents relating to the agreement were ever signed

Rachel wrote and signed a letter to Sarah, which stated that: ‘I have sold my interest in the family trust to Brian save for Bleak House, which I am holding on trust for your daughter, Karen’.

Rachel died last month. Her validly executed will provides that her entire estate is bequeathed to several charities.  What rights, if any, do Brian and Karen have in the family trust? 

!ANSWER
K retains her beneficial interest because in this case Rachel drops out but gives her beneficial interest in one asset, the house to K. Technically speaking R was not holding the house on trust but had transferred her beneficial interest to K. Non habet rule, R had no legal interest and only had a beneficial interest. 

Therefore the evidential requirements are those of 

'A declaration of trust respecting any land or any interests therin must be manifested and proved by some writing signed by some person who is able to declare such trust or by his will,' 

BUT! This relates to new trusts, there is no new trust being created, merely the beneficiary of the trust has shifted around. 

!THEREFORE

S.53(1)(c) is the relevant statute

'A disposition of an equitable interest or trust subsisting at the time of the disposition must be in writing signed by the person disposing of the same or by his agent there unto lawfully authorized in writing or by will.'

Writing requirement would be satisfied by the letter that R signed, and the trust would exist from the time of the letter being signed. 

The position that has emerged confirmed in Neville v. Wilson -> S.53(1)(c) does not need to be complied with in a sub trust dropping out situation. S.53(1)(c) does not apply to the creation of new interests only with the transfer of subsisting interests. 

Given that R had no interest at the time of death then the interest in the house given to K would remain with K, though the validity period would start to count down with the death. Perpetuity rule applied. 

B retains the beneficial interest over the shares that B purchased, as well as retaining some beneficial interest in the house contingent on the settlement and distribution to R and B in the declaration of the original family trust.
