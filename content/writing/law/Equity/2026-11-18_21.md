You can create a trust over some property without any formalities, you can declare a trust over chattel by declaring it and then transferring it to a trustee, but otherwise you need to comply with formalities. 

It is contingent on the type of property.

Evidential, symbolic and cautionary are the three functions of a trust. Enables the trustee and the beneficiary to establish the whereabouts of the property and ascertain who has the legal and equitable interests over it. Certain property might also have a lot of societal valuee. The transfer of land for an example and the symbolism of formalities reflects the importance of those assets. This is a general category. Also cautionary and think about the extreme levels of obligation. Making people think before proceeding.

Tax considerations. Something they can tax. 

Can people create things without formalities may allow avoidance of taxes. Inter vivos formalities. Post mortem formalities are not what we're concerned with today. Secret trusts. 

S.53 Law of Property Act 1(b) and 1(c) and also a little bit of 1(a)

'A declaration of trust respecting any land or any interests therin must be manifested and proved by some writing signed by some person who is able to declare such trust or by his will,' 

Three things

1. Concerns the declaration of trust so express trust

2. Must be evidenced

3. Must be signed by a qualified person

The same as the prior provision, in the statute of frauds. 

Rochefold v. Boustead -> Mortaged land to a mortgagee who sold it to D. Orally agreed D would hold on trust for mortgagor, D sold land to third party in breach of an agreement. Oral agreement but no evidence. D then sold that land on without mentioning the mortgage. The court allowed an exception in this case and held that they would admit oral evidence that the trust of land had been created in spite of noncompliance with statutory provision. 

'Equity will not allow statute to be used as a cloak for fraud.'

In 1677 there was a concern regarding the risk of fraud for those who owned land. Legal owners were not absolutely entitled. Rather trustees, false allegations founded on parole evidence. One word against another. Legal owners needed protection. 

Equity will sometimes disapply statute.

S.53(1)(b)

'must be manifested and proved by some writing signed by some person who is able to declare such a trust,' 

Look at the evidence to support the declaration of trust. It does not need to be at the same time as the trust, but evidence after it is established or even valid. 

Ong v. Ping -> In that case the written evidence signed by a qualifying person had been signed 2 years after the trust had been created. 

S.53(1)(b) not a validity requirement. It is classically an evidential requirement. It's not about if a trust exists or not. 

Non-compliance with S.53(1)(b) renders a trust of land 'valid but unenforceable,' distinction between validity and evidential requirement. 

Trust of imperfect obligation would be the anomalous exceptions like Re Endracott. They are valid but they're not enforceable without equity's intervention. 

Can law exist without enforcement? That is the position. Even if it is unenforceable the beneficiaries have rights, and trustees have responsibilities which a court may find.

Nature of trusteeship 'In the case of a trust the conscience of the owner requires him to carry out the purposes for which the property was vested in him or which the law imposes on him by reason of his unconscionable conduct.' From 'Westdeusche v. Islington BC.' 

Equity will intervene if there is unconsciconable conduct. They will treat someone as a trustee as a result of their behaviour. Can arise without consent as a result of the wrongdoing and the conscience.

Sold to brother in law on oral understanding that they would be sold under market value that she would be able to live there for the rest of her days. S.53(1)(c) 

'A disposition of an equitable interest or trust subsisting at the time of the disposition must be in writing signed by the person disposing of the same or by hish agent there unto lawfully authorized in writing or by will.'

This is a substantive and not evidential provision. The transfer is void but for compliance with this section. 

Next a disposition is a transfer and importantly we're talking about subsisting equitable interests. Those that are already in existence. Passage or transfer of already existing interests. 

Final point to mention is the type of property is broadly construed. Shares have been caught, it is any equitable interest over a range of property not just land. 

The courts have gone against this in various ways. 

1. Preventing fraud

'To prevent hidden oral transactions in equitable interests in fraud of those truly entitled making it difficult if not impossible for the trustees to ascertain who are in truth the beneficiaries,' 

Lord Upjohn in Re Vandervell v. IRC

Why are these interests vulnerable to fraud? Normally there is a lot of documentation but equitable interests can be transferred informally with no documentation. So the concern is that there may be informal transfers which cannot be ascertained and people may be defrauded of their interests. We can trace who owns what. 

C transfers equitable interest to D, does S.53(1)(c) need be complied with, or not? They do because Lord Upjohn there needs to be signed writing to avoid fraud and allow something to be traced.

Sub trust is the next  C holds 100 shares on trust for D, D declares a trust for the benefit of E. Does a sub trust come into existence or does D drop out. Does S.53(1)(c) need to be complied with. In the old case law, they just drop out. 

The position that has emerged confirmed in Neville v. Wilson -> S.53(1)(c) does not need to be complied with in a sub trust dropping out situation. S.53(1)(c) does not apply to the creation of new interests only with the transfer of subsisting interests. 

Grey v. IRC -> The key point there was a settlor and had 6 grandchildren and 18,000 shares, wanted to create 6 trusts one for each grandchild and wants to avoid tax liability. Went through a lot of steps. Oral declaration to transfer his beneficial interest to 6 trusts for his grandchildren, confirmed through deeds subsequently executed, so when did the transfer happen. At the moment of the oral instruction then no tax liability or when the deeds were executed then they would attract tax liabilities. HoL decided that the relevant moment of transfer was in March when the deeds were executed. Court decided that the key thing that is important for S.53(1)(c) is separate movements of legal and equitable title. The second step was the event that separated the titles. Where the legal and equitable titles have been separated that is when the requirement is engaged. If they move together it is easier to see. 

Vandervell v. IRC -> Huge series of cases involving a successful entrepeneur, and at one point he had an F-1 team. A lot of money. He wanted to establish a chair of pharmacology at the royal college of surgeons. Wanted to do this in a tax efficient way. He had shares in a company. He was the beneficiary of these shares. He instructed the bank orally to transfer the legal and equitable title to the royal college of surgeons. The plan was that he would give them for a period of time, and they would produce money, dividends. Value would accrue to the college. Dividends of 250k and that was enough to fund the chair. An arrangement whereby those shares would be pulled back. Did Tony have to produced signed writing to validly affect this transfer? If it was not a valid transfer then he would be liable for income tax on the dividends. What did HoL find? No there was no need to comply with S.53(1)(c) only when a subsisting equitable interest is moved independently does it need to be complied with. Another argument put forward. 

Equity will sometimes dispense with formalities.
